The **Vigenère cipher** is a classic method of encrypting alphabetic text by using a series of different Caesar ciphers based
on the letters of a keyword. Invented in the 16th century, it was famously known for centuries as **_le chiffre
indéchiffrable_** (the "indecipherable cipher") because it successfully resisted codebreaking attempts until the 19th
century.

---

### How It Works: The Vigenère Square

The cipher relies on a table known as the **Vigenère square** (or _Tabula Recta_), which consists of the alphabet written out
26 times in rows, each row shifted slightly to the left compared to the previous one.

Instead of substituting every 'A' with the same letter (like a standard Caesar cipher), the Vigenère cipher changes the
substitution alphabet depending on a repeating **keyword**.

---

### Step-by-Step Encryption Example

Let's encrypt the plaintext **`ATTACK`** using the keyword **`LEMON`**.

1. **Repeat the keyword** to match the length of the plaintext:

- Plaintext: `A T T A C K`
- Keyword: `L E M O N L`

2. **Find the intersection** of each plaintext letter (column) and keyword letter (row) on the Vigenère square:

- **A** (column) + **L** (row) = **L**
- **T** (column) + **E** (row) = **X**
- **T** (column) + **M** (row) = **F**
- **A** (column) + **O** (row) = **O**
- **C** (column) + **N** (row) = **P**
- **K** (column) + **L** (row) = **V**

- **Ciphertext:** `LXFOPV`

To decrypt, the receiver uses the same keyword, finds the row corresponding to the keyword letter, looks across that row to
find the ciphertext letter, and reads the column header to get the original plaintext letter.

---

### History and How It Was Broken

- **Origins:** First described by Giovan Battista Bellaso in 1553, the cipher was later incorrectly attributed to French
  diplomat Blaise de Vigenère in the 19th century, which is how it got its modern name.
- **The Crack:** For nearly 300 years, cryptanalysts failed to break it. However, in 1863, a Prussian military officer named
  Friedrich Kasiski published a method (the **Kasiski examination**) that looked for repeating patterns in the ciphertext to
  figure out the length of the keyword, eventually allowing codebreakers to break it down into multiple monoalphabetic
  ciphers and solve it.
