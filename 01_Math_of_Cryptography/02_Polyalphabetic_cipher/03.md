Breaking the Vigenère cipher—long considered the "indecipherable cipher"—changed the course of cryptography. Because a
polyalphabetic cipher shifts letters using a repeating keyword, codebreakers realized they couldn't attack it all at once.
Instead, the strategy required a two-step process: **first, find the length of the keyword**, and **second, break it down
into individual Caesar ciphers**.

---

### Step 1: Finding the Keyword Length

The breakthrough came from noticing that if the same word in the plaintext happens to align with the same part of the keyword
multiple times, it will produce the **exact same ciphertext sequence**.

- **The Kasiski Examination:** Published by Friedrich Kasiski in 1863, this method looks for identical groups of three or
  more letters repeating in the ciphertext.
- **Analyzing Distances:** Codebreakers measure the distance (number of characters) between these repeating sequences.
  Because the keyword must repeat to match the text, the distance between identical ciphertext patterns is almost always a
  **multiple of the keyword length**. By finding common divisors among multiple distances, the exact length of the keyword
  can be deduced.

---

### Step 2: The Index of Coincidence (The Friedman Test)

If a ciphertext doesn't have obvious repeating patterns, cryptanalysts use William F. Friedman’s **Index of Coincidence
(IC)**.

- The Index of Coincidence measures how likely it is to randomly pick two identical letters from a text.
- Standard English text has a predictable IC (~0.067) because letters like 'E' and 'T' appear much more often than 'Z' or
  'Q'.
- A monoalphabetic cipher preserves this high IC, but a polyalphabetic cipher flattens the letter distribution, dropping the
  IC closer to random text (~0.038). By testing different hypothetical keyword lengths, codebreakers can look for spikes
  where the IC returns to normal English levels, revealing the correct keyword length.

---

### Step 3: Frequency Analysis on Separated Streams

Once the keyword length ($n$) is known (for example, $n = 5$), breaking the cipher becomes trivial:

1. **Split the Ciphertext:** The ciphertext is divided into $n$ separate columns or streams. Every 5th letter goes into its
   own group.
2. **Treat as Caesar Ciphers:** Because each stream was encrypted using a single, constant letter of the keyword, each stream
   is actually just a standard **Caesar cipher**.
3. **Apply Frequency Analysis:** Codebreakers analyze the letter frequencies of each stream. By matching the most frequent
   letter in a stream to the most frequent letter in English (**'E'**), they can instantly calculate the shift—and thus
   discover that specific letter of the keyword.

Repeating this for all streams reveals the full keyword, allowing the entire message to be decrypted instantly.
