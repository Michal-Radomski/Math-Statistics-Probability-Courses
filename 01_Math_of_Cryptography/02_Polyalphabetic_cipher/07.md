Calculating **probability** allows you to measure mathematically how likely it is that a specific event will occur.
Probabilities always fall on a scale from **0** (impossible) to **1** (certain), or from $0\%$ to $100\%$.

---

### The Basic Formula

At its core, calculating simple probability relies on a straightforward ratio:

$$P(\text{Event}) = \frac{\text{Number of Favorable Outcomes}}{\text{Total Number of Possible Outcomes}}$$

- **Favorable Outcomes:** The specific results you are looking for or hoping to measure.
- **Total Possible Outcomes:** Every single possible result that could happen in that scenario.

---

### Step-by-Step Example: Rolling a Die

Imagine you roll a standard 6-sided die. What is the probability of rolling an **even number**?

1. **Identify the total possible outcomes:** A die has 6 sides, so there are 6 possible numbers you can roll:
   $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$. ($Total = 6$)
2. **Identify the favorable outcomes:** You want an even number. The even numbers on a die are $\{2, 4, 6\}$.
   ($Favorable = 3$)
3. **Apply the formula:**

$$P(\text{Even Number}) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5 \text{ (or } 50\% \text{)}$$

There is a $50\%$ chance of rolling an even number.

---

### The Rule of Complements (Opposites)

Sometimes it is easier to calculate the probability of something _not_ happening. The probability of an event happening plus
the probability of it _not_ happening always equals $1$ ($100\%$):

$$P(\text{Not A}) = 1 - P(\text{A})$$

For example, if the probability of rain today is $0.2$ ($20\%$), the probability that it _won't_ rain is:

$$1 - 0.2 = 0.8 \text{ (or } 80\% \text{)}$$

---

The probability of the intersection (often called the **product rule** or the probability of "$A$ **and** $B$") measures the
likelihood that **both** events occur at the same time. It is denoted as $P(A \cap B)$ or $P(A \text{ and } B)$.

How you calculate this depends entirely on whether the two events are **independent** or **dependent**.

---

### Case 1: Independent Events

Two events are **independent** if the outcome of the first event has **no effect** on the outcome of the second event (like
rolling a die and flipping a coin).

- **The Formula:**

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$

- **Example:** What is the probability of rolling a `6` on a standard 6-sided die **and** getting heads (`H`) on a coin flip?
- Probability of rolling a 6: $P(A) = \frac{1}{6}$
- Probability of getting heads: $P(B) = \frac{1}{2}$
- **Intersection:** $P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{12}$ (or $\approx 8.3\%$)

---

### Case 2: Dependent Events

Two events are **dependent** if the outcome of the first event **changes** the probability of the second event (like drawing
cards from a deck _without_ putting them back).

- **The Formula:**

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A)$$

*(Note: $P(B \mid A)$ means "the probability of event $B$ *given that* event $A$ has already occurred.")*

- **Example:** What is the probability of drawing **two Aces in a row** from a standard 52-card deck without replacement?
- **First Card:** The probability of drawing an Ace is $P(A) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}$.
- **Second Card:** Since one Ace is gone, there are now only 3 Aces left out of 51 total cards. The probability is
  $P(B \mid A) = \frac{3}{51} = \frac{1}{17}$.
- **Intersection:** $\frac{4}{52} \times \frac{3}{51} = \frac{12}{2652} = \frac{1}{221}$ (or $\approx 0.45\%$)

---

### Special Case: Mutually Exclusive Events

If two events are **mutually exclusive**—meaning they **cannot** happen at the same time (like rolling both a `4` and a `5`
on a single die roll)—their intersection is always **$0$**.

$$P(A \cap B) = 0$$
