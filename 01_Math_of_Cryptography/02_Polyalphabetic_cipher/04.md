The **Index of Coincidence (IC)** is a statistical measure used in cryptography to analyze the letter frequencies of a text.
Invented by cryptanalyst William F. Friedman in 1920, it is one of the most powerful tools for breaking polyalphabetic
ciphers (like the Vigenère cipher) by helping codebreakers determine the length of an unknown keyword.

---

### What Does "Coincidence" Mean?

Imagine taking a message, writing it out, and then writing an identical copy right below it. If you slide the bottom copy to
the right by a few positions and count how many letters line up vertically (coincide), you are measuring coincidence.

More formally, the Index of Coincidence calculates the **probability that two randomly chosen letters from a given text are
the exact same letter**.

---

### The Mathematical Formula

The Index of Coincidence is calculated using the following formula:

$$IC = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{26} n_i(n_i - 1)}{N(N - 1)}$$

- **$N$** = The total length of the text (number of letters).
- **$n_i$** = The frequency of the $i$-th letter of the alphabet (from $A$ to $Z$) in the text.
- **$\sum$** = The sum across all 26 letters of the alphabet.
- **$N(N - 1)$** = The total number of possible pairs of letters that can be drawn from the text.

---

### Why Languages Have a Predictable IC

Natural languages are not random; certain letters appear much more frequently than others (in English, **E**, **T**, **A**,
and **O** dominate, while **Z**, **Q**, and **X** are rare).

Because of this uneven distribution, if you pick two random letters from an English text, there is a relatively high chance
they will both be 'E's or another common letter.

- **English Plaintext IC:** Approximately **$0.067$** (or $6.7\%$)
- **Random Text / Uniform Distribution IC:** Approximately **$0.038$** (or $3.8\%$) — because every letter appears roughly
  equally often, making exact matches much rarer.

---

### How It Breaks Polyalphabetic Ciphers

When a message is encrypted with a monoalphabetic cipher (like a Caesar cipher), the letter distribution is shifted, but the
unevenness remains. Therefore, the IC of a monoalphabetic ciphertext remains close to **$0.067$**.

However, a polyalphabetic cipher (like Vigenère) scrambles the text across multiple alphabets, flattening the letter
distribution and dropping the IC down toward **$0.038$** (resembling random noise).

Cryptanalysts use this drop to their advantage:

1. They test hypothetical keyword lengths (e.g., trying length 1, 2, 3, etc.).
2. They split the ciphertext into columns based on that guessed length.
3. They calculate the IC for each column.
4. When the guessed length matches the _actual_ keyword length, each column aligns with a single Caesar shift, causing the IC
   to **spike back up to $0.067$**, revealing the correct keyword length instantly.
