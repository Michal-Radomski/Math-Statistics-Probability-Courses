The **Friedman test** (sometimes called the **Kappa test**) is a statistical method introduced by legendary cryptanalyst
William F. Friedman in 1922. It provides a direct, mathematical way to estimate the length of an unknown keyword in a
polyalphabetic cipher (such as the Vigenère cipher).

While the Kasiski examination relies on finding lucky repeating text patterns in the ciphertext, the Friedman test uses pure
statistics, making it effective even when a ciphertext is relatively short or lacks obvious repeated words.

---

### The Core Logic

The test relies on how a polyalphabetic cipher affects the **Index of Coincidence (IC)**:

- **English Plaintext IC ($I_r$)**: $\approx 0.067$
- **Random Text / Uniform Distribution IC ($I_p$)**: $\approx 0.038$

When a message is encrypted with a keyword of length $L$, the ciphertext is effectively split into $L$ separate interleaved
streams, where each stream acts like a standard monoalphabetic Caesar cipher.

Because characters within the same stream share the same shift, they retain some of the uneven letter frequencies of natural
language. By measuring the overall IC of the ciphertext, you can mathematically determine how many independent streams ($L$)
were mixed together.

---

### The Friedman Formula

To estimate the keyword length ($L$), the Friedman test uses the following formula, which compares the observed IC of the
ciphertext against the expected IC values of English text and random text:

$$L \approx \frac{0.027 \times N}{(N - 1)\text{IC} - 0.038N + 0.065}$$

- **$N$** = The total length of the ciphertext.
- **$\text{IC}$** = The measured Index of Coincidence of the ciphertext.
- **$0.027$** (approximately $0.067 - 0.040$) and the other constants represent the expected statistical baselines for the
  English alphabet.

---

### How Cryptanalysts Use It

1. **Calculate the Ciphertext IC:** Compute the Index of Coincidence for the entire encrypted message using the standard IC
   formula.
2. **Apply the Formula:** Plug the ciphertext length ($N$) and the calculated IC into Friedman’s formula to solve for $L$.
3. **Round to the Nearest Whole Number:** Because key lengths must be whole integers, the result will point directly to the
   likely length of the keyword (e.g., if the formula outputs `4.92`, the keyword is almost certainly 5 letters long).
4. **Proceed to Decryption:** Once the key length is known, the ciphertext is split into columns, and frequency analysis is
   used to recover each individual letter of the keyword.
