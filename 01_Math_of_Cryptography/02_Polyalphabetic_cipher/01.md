A **polyalphabetic cipher** is a type of substitution cipher that uses multiple different substitution alphabets to encrypt a
message. Unlike a _monoalphabetic_ cipher (such as the Caesar cipher), which uses a single fixed alphabet throughout the
entire message, a polyalphabetic cipher switches between different alphabets based on a system or a keyword.

This method was historically invented to make ciphers much harder to crack by breaking **frequency analysis**—a technique
where codebreakers look for how often certain letters appear in a message to guess the plain text.

---

### How It Works

In a polyalphabetic cipher, the same letter in the plaintext can be encrypted into different ciphertext letters depending on
where it appears in the message or what key is being used at that moment.

For example, the letter **'E'** might be encrypted as **'X'** the first time it appears, **'Q'** the second time, and **'M'**
the third time. This flattens out the letter frequency distribution, making it nearly impossible for codebreakers to use
standard language patterns to decrypt it easily.

---

### Famous Examples

1. **The Vigenère Cipher:** Often called the most famous polyalphabetic cipher, it uses a keyword to determine which Caesar
   cipher alphabet to use for each letter of the message. If the keyword is `KEY`, the first letter of the message is shifted
   using the alphabet for 'K', the second by 'E', the third by 'Y', and then the pattern repeats.
2. **The Alberti Cipher Disc:** Invented by Leon Battista Alberti in the 15th century, this was one of the earliest
   polyalphabetic systems. It used two concentric metal discs containing different alphabets that could be rotated relative
   to each other during encryption to change the substitution alphabet.
3. **The Enigma Machine:** Used by Germany during World War II, the Enigma was an electromechanical rotor machine that
   implemented a complex, constantly changing polyalphabetic substitution system. Every time a key was pressed, the internal
   rotors shifted, ensuring that pressing the same letter twice would result in two completely different ciphertext letters.

---

### Why It Was a Breakthrough

Before polyalphabetic ciphers, almost all ciphers could be easily broken using frequency analysis because languages have
predictable letter frequencies (for instance, 'E' is the most common letter in English). By constantly changing the
substitution alphabet, polyalphabetic ciphers hid these patterns, keeping secrets much safer until the advent of advanced
statistical cryptanalysis and computers.
