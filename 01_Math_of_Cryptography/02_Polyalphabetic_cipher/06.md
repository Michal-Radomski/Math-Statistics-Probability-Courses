The **one-time pad (OTP)**—sometimes called the Vernam cipher—is an encryption technique that is mathematically proven to be
**unbreakable**. Unlike other ciphers (like the Vigenère or even modern AES), a properly implemented one-time pad offers
_perfect secrecy_, meaning even an attacker with infinite computing power cannot decrypt the ciphertext without the key.

---

### How It Works

The mechanics of a one-time pad are remarkably simple. It relies on combining the plaintext message with a secret random key
using modular arithmetic (usually the **XOR** operation in binary).

1. **The Key:** The sender and receiver share a secret key that consists of truly random data.
2. **Encryption:** Each character (or bit) of the message is combined with the corresponding character of the key.
3. **Decryption:** The receiver applies the exact same key to the ciphertext, which reverses the operation and recovers the
   original message.

Because the key is combined directly with the text, the ciphertext looks like pure, structureless random noise. There are no
patterns, repeating keywords, or statistical flaws to exploit.

---

### The Three Strict Rules

For a one-time pad to achieve true perfect secrecy, it must strictly follow three rules:

1. **The key must be truly random:** It cannot be generated by a predictable computer algorithm (pseudorandom); it must come
   from physical random processes (like atmospheric noise or radioactive decay).
2. **The key must be at least as long as the message:** If a message is 1 megabyte long, the key must also be at least 1
   megabyte long.
3. **The key must never be reused:** A key can only be used _once_ (hence the name). If you encrypt two different messages
   with the same pad, an attacker can mathematically compare the two ciphertexts and strip away the randomness to read both
   messages.

---

### Why It's Unbreakable (Perfect Secrecy)

In 1949, mathematician Claude Shannon mathematically proved that the one-time pad provides absolute security.

Imagine you intercept a ciphertext that reads `XJ7#m`. Because the key is completely random and as long as the message, that
exact ciphertext could decrypt to _any_ possible message of the same length (e.g., "Attack at dawn" or "Meet me for lunch")
depending on what key you apply. An attacker has no way of knowing which interpretation is correct, leaving them with zero
clues.

---

### The Practical Catch

If it's unbreakable, why don't we use it for everything (like securing the internet)?

- **The Key Distribution Problem:** To send a 10 GB file securely using a one-time pad, you and the recipient must first
  securely share a 10 GB random key. If you already have a secure way to transmit a massive key across the world, you could
  have just used that channel to send your message in the first place!
- **Logistics:** Managing, storing, and destroying massive physical keys makes it impractical for everyday civilian use,
  though it has historically been used for extreme high-level diplomatic and military communications (such as the Cold War
  Washington-Moscow hotline).
