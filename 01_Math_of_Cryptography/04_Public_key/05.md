The **Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT)** is a famous result from number theory that allows you to solve a system of
simultaneous modular equations, even when the numbers involved seem disconnected.

Originally stated by the ancient Chinese mathematician Sunzi in the 3rd century AD, it has surprising practical applications
today—from scheduling recurring events to speeding up modern cryptography.

---

## The Intuition: Sunzi’s Ancient Puzzle

To understand how the theorem works, look at the classic puzzle Sunzi posed:

> _"When things are counted by threes, the remainder is 2; by fives, the remainder is 3; by sevens, the remainder is 2. What
> is the number?"_

Expressed as modular math, we are looking for an unknown number $x$ that satisfies three rules at once:

1. $x \equiv 2 \pmod 3$
2. $x \equiv 3 \pmod 5$
3. $x \equiv 2 \pmod 7$

If you test numbers, you will find that **23** is a solution:

- $23 \div 3 = 7$ with a remainder of **2**
- $23 \div 5 = 4$ with a remainder of **3**
- $23 \div 7 = 3$ with a remainder of **2**

In fact, any number of the form $23 + (3 \times 5 \times 7)k$ (where $k$ is any integer) will also work, meaning the
solutions repeat every 105 numbers. The Chinese Remainder Theorem proves that a unique solution _always_ exists within that
range as long as the divisors (3, 5, and 7) share no common factors other than 1 (they are **coprime**).

---

## The Mathematical Definition

Formally, suppose you have a set of equations:

- $x \equiv a_1 \pmod{m_1}$
- $x \equiv a_2 \pmod{m_2}$
- $\vdots$
- $x \equiv a_k \pmod{m_k}$

If all the moduli ($m_1, m_2, \dots, m_k$) are pairwise coprime (meaning no two share a common divisor), the theorem
guarantees that there is **one unique solution** for $x$ modulo $M$, where $M$ is the product of all the moduli
($M = m_1 \times m_2 \times \dots \times m_k$).

---

## Why Is It So Important in Computer Science?

While it started as a mathematical puzzle, the Chinese Remainder Theorem is a powerhouse in modern computing, particularly in
**RSA decryption**.

When a computer decrypts an RSA message, it has to compute massive numbers raised to large powers modulo $n$ ($n$ is the
product of two huge primes, $p$ and $q$). Doing this directly is computationally heavy and slow.

Instead, the **CRT optimization** splits the problem into two smaller parts:

1. Compute the result modulo $p$.
2. Compute the result modulo $q$.
3. Use the Chinese Remainder Theorem to stitch the two smaller results back together into the final answer.

Because arithmetic on smaller numbers is exponentially faster than arithmetic on massive numbers, using CRT can make RSA
decryption **up to 4 times faster** on computer processors.
