**Elliptic-Curve Diffie-Hellman (ECDH)** is a modern variant of the classic Diffie-Hellman key exchange. Like standard
Diffie-Hellman, it allows two parties to securely establish a shared secret over an untrusted public network without ever
transmitting the secret itself.

However, ECDH replaces traditional modular arithmetic with the mathematics of **elliptic curves**, making it vastly more
efficient and the backbone of modern secure communications (such as HTTPS, TLS 1.3, and end-to-end encrypted messaging apps
like WhatsApp and Signal).

---

## 1. Why Elliptic Curves? (The Power of Small Keys)

Traditional Diffie-Hellman relies on prime numbers and modular exponentiation. To achieve high security today, it requires
massive numbers (typically 2048-bit keys or larger).

ECDH achieves the **exact same level of security with much smaller keys** (typically 256-bit keys).

- **Speed & Efficiency:** Smaller keys mean less data to transmit and much faster computations. This is why smartphones,
  smartwatches, and low-power IoT devices can encrypt your traffic effortlessly.
- **The Underlying Problem:** ECDH relies on the **Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP)**. While it is very easy
  for a computer to "multiply" a point on a curve by a secret number, it is computationally impossible to reverse the process
  to find the secret number if you only know the starting and ending points.

---

## 2. How ECDH Works Step-by-Step

Instead of mixing colors or using modular exponentiation, ECDH uses **scalar point multiplication** on a curved line defined
by an algebraic equation.

1. **Public Setup:**

- Both parties agree on a specific **elliptic curve** and a standard starting point on that curve called the **Base Point
  ($G$)**. These parameters are public.

2. **Private Keys:**

- **Alice** chooses a secret random number $a$ (her private key).
- **Bob** chooses a secret random number $b$ (his private key).

3. **Public Key Generation:**

- Alice calculates her public key by adding the base point $G$ to itself $a$ times on the curve: $A = a \times G$. She sends
  $A$ to Bob.
- Bob calculates his public key similarly: $B = b \times G$. He sends $B$ to Alice.

4. **Computing the Shared Secret:**

- Alice takes Bob's public key and multiplies it by her private key: $S = a \times B$.
- Bob takes Alice's public key and multiplies it by his private key: $S = b \times A$.
- Because of the commutative property of elliptic curve math ($a \times (b \times G) = b \times (a \times G)$), **both Alice
  and Bob arrive at the exact same shared secret point ($S$)**.

An eavesdropper seeing public keys $A$ and $B$ flying across the network cannot deduce private keys $a$ or $b$, nor can they
compute the shared secret $S$.

---

## Where Is ECDH Used?

Because of its speed and small footprint, ECDH is everywhere today:

- **HTTPS/TLS:** Securing virtually all modern web traffic when you visit secure websites.
- **Secure Messaging:** Protecting chats in Signal, WhatsApp, and iMessage.
- **Cryptocurrencies:** Used in Bitcoin and other blockchain networks for signing transactions and deriving wallet addresses.
