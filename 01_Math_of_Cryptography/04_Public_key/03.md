The **RSA algorithm** is one of the most famous and foundational public-key cryptosystems in history. Invented in 1977 by Ron
**R**ivest, Adi **S**hamir, and Leonard **A**dleman (hence **RSA**), it revolutionized security by solving a fundamental
problem: how can two people securely exchange messages without ever meeting to share a secret key beforehand?

Like Diffie-Hellman, RSA relies on a **one-way function**—a mathematical operation that is very easy to do in one direction,
but computationally impossible to reverse unless you have a specific piece of secret information.

---

## 1. The Padlock Analogy (How Public-Key Cryptography Works)

To understand RSA intuitively, imagine a physical postal system using padlocks:

1. **The Open Padlock (Public Key):** You want anyone to be able to send you secret letters. You leave an open padlock with
   your name on it out in the public square. Anyone can take your open padlock, snap it shut on a box containing their
   message, and lock it.
2. **The Private Key:** Once the box is locked with your public padlock, **only you** have the tiny physical key that can
   open it. Even the person who locked the box can no longer open it.
3. **Security:** Anyone can _lock_ a message to you (using your public key), but only you can _unlock_ it (using your private
   key).

In digital terms, your **public key** is shared with the world, while your **private key** is kept secret on your device.

---

## 2. The Math Behind RSA: The Trapdoor of Prime Numbers

While Diffie-Hellman relies on discrete logarithms, RSA relies heavily on the **Integer Factorization Problem**:

- **Easy Direction:** Multiplying two large prime numbers together ($11 \times 13 = 143$) takes a fraction of a millisecond
  for a computer.
- **Hard Direction:** If someone gives you the number `143` and asks you to find the original prime numbers that created it,
  you have to factor it. For small numbers, this is easy. But for modern RSA, the numbers used are hundreds of digits long.
  Multiplying them takes milliseconds, but factoring the resulting product back into its original primes would take even the
  most powerful supercomputers millions of years.

This asymmetry is the "trapdoor" that secures your data.

## 3. How RSA Works Step-by-Step

### Step 1: Key Generation

1. Choose two distinct, very large random prime numbers, let's call them $p$ and $q$.
2. Multiply them together to find a modulus $n$:

$$n = p \times q$$

(The number $n$ becomes part of both the public and private keys; its length is the "key size," such as 2048 bits). 3.
Compute a mathematical value called Euler's totient, $\phi(n) = (p - 1)(q - 1)$. 4. Choose a public exponent $e$ (commonly
`65537`). 5. Compute the private exponent $d$ using modular arithmetic such that $d$ is the inverse of $e$ modulo $\phi(n)$.

- **Public Key:** $(e, n)$ — Distributed freely.
- **Private Key:** $(d, n)$ — Kept strictly secret.

### Step 2: Encryption

When Alice wants to send a secret message $M$ to Bob:

1. She turns her message into a number.
2. She encrypts it using Bob's public key $(e, n)$ to create the ciphertext $C$:

$$C = M^e \pmod n$$

3. She sends $C$ over the internet. Anyone can see $C$, but without Bob's private key, it looks like gibberish.

### Step 3: Decryption

When Bob receives the ciphertext $C$, he uses his private key $d$ to unlock the message:

$$M = C^d \pmod n$$

Because only Bob knows $d$, only Bob can recover the original message $M$.

## Where Is RSA Used Today?

For decades, RSA was the undisputed king of web security. Today, while it is still widely used for **digital signatures**
(verifying that software updates or website certificates are authentic), its role in encryption has largely been taken over
or supplemented by **Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)**.

Because ECC achieves the same level of security with significantly smaller keys, it is much faster and more efficient for
modern smartphones, web browsers, and battery-powered devices.
