The future of cryptography is defined by a massive, high-stakes transition: preparing the world’s digital infrastructure for
the arrival of quantum computing.

While classical cryptography (like RSA and Elliptic-Curve Cryptography) has successfully secured the internet for decades,
the rise of cryptographically relevant quantum computers threatens to break these underlying mathematical assumptions. As a
result, cryptographic science is undergoing its most profound transformation in history.

The future of cryptography centers on several major pillars:

### 1. Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC)

Quantum computers will be able to run Shor’s algorithm, which can efficiently solve the prime factorization and discrete
logarithm problems that secure modern public-key cryptography. To counteract this, NIST (the National Institute of Standards
and Technology) has finalized and standardized **Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC)** algorithms.

- **Lattice-Based Cryptography:** The primary mathematical approach moving forward relies on high-dimensional lattices (such
  as **ML-KEM** for key encapsulation and **ML-DSA** for digital signatures). These problems are hard for both classical and
  quantum computers to solve.
- **Stateful Hash-Based Signatures:** Algorithms like **SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+)** serve as robust alternatives for digital
  signatures, relying on the security of hash functions rather than algebraic structures.

### 2. Defending Against "Harvest Now, Decrypt Later"

The quantum threat is not just a future problem; it is happening right now. Adversaries and nation-states are actively
intercepting and storing encrypted sensitive data (traffic logs, state secrets, financial communications) today. Even though
current quantum computers cannot break this data yet, attackers plan to decrypt it retroactively once powerful quantum
computers become a reality. This has made migrating to quantum-resistant standards an urgent priority rather than a distant
roadmap item.

### 3. Cryptographic Agility

Historically, changing a core cryptographic algorithm took decades because protocols were hard-coded into software and
hardware. The future relies heavily on **crypto-agility**—the design capability of systems to seamlessly swap out underlying
cryptographic algorithms, keys, and protocols via automated policies without requiring major structural re-engineering.

### 4. Advanced Privacy-Enhancing Technologies (PETs)

Beyond resisting quantum threats, cryptography is evolving to handle data utility and privacy in revolutionary ways:

- **Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs):** Allowing one party to prove to another that a statement is true without revealing any
  information beyond the validity of the statement itself. ZKPs are transforming secure authentication, digital identity
  (like zk-passports), and scalable blockchain scaling.
- **Homomorphic Encryption:** Allowing computations to be performed directly on encrypted data without ever decrypting it
  first. This enables secure cloud computing, where sensitive healthcare or financial data can be analyzed by third parties
  or AI models while remaining entirely private.

### 5. What Remains Safe?

Interestingly, not everything needs to be replaced. **Symmetric encryption algorithms** (like AES-256) and **cryptographic
hash functions** (like SHA-2 and SHA-3) are already considered largely quantum-resistant. While quantum algorithms like
Grover's algorithm weaken symmetric keys (effectively cutting their security strength in half, requiring a move to 256-bit
keys), they do not break them outright. The primary focus of the global migration is entirely on **public-key cryptography**
(key exchanges and digital signatures).
