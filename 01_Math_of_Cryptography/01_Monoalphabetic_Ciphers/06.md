The **Euclidean algorithm** is an ancient, remarkably efficient method for finding the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) of two
numbers. Published by the Greek mathematician Euclid around 300 BC, it avoids the tedious process of listing out every single
factor of both numbers.

Instead, it uses a clever repeating pattern of division and remainders to zero in on the answer in just a few steps.

---

### 1. The Core Principle

Euclid's algorithm relies on a foundational property of numbers: **The greatest common divisor of two numbers also divides
their remainder.**

Mathematically, for any two integers $a$ and $b$ (where $a > b$):

$$\text{GCD}(a, b) = \text{GCD}(b, a \pmod b)$$

You keep dividing the numbers and replacing them with their remainders until the remainder becomes **$0$**. The last non-zero
remainder you get is your GCD.

---

### 2. Step-by-Step Example

Let’s find the Greatest Common Divisor of **252** and **105** using Euclid’s algorithm.

- **Step 1:** Divide the larger number ($252$) by the smaller number ($105$).
- $252 \div 105 = 2$ with a **remainder of 42**.
- _(In equation form: $252 = (2 \times 105) + 42$)_
- Now, our new problem is to find $\text{GCD}(105, 42)$.

- **Step 2:** Take the old divisor ($105$) and divide it by the new remainder ($42$).
- $105 \div 42 = 2$ with a **remainder of 21**.
- _(In equation form: $105 = (2 \times 42) + 21$)_
- Now, find $\text{GCD}(42, 21)$.

- **Step 3:** Take the previous divisor ($42$) and divide it by the new remainder ($21$).
- $42 \div 21 = 2$ with a **remainder of 0**.
- _(In equation form: $42 = (2 \times 21) + 0$)_

Since the remainder has reached **$0$**, we stop! **The last non-zero remainder ($21$) is our GCD.**

Therefore, $\text{GCD}(252, 105) = 21$.

---

### 3. Why Is It So Important?

- **Blazing Speed:** Even if you give the algorithm numbers that are hundreds of digits long, it computes the GCD almost
  instantly.
- **The Backbone of Cryptography:** In public-key cryptography (like RSA), computers need to instantly verify whether giant
  numbers share any common factors (i.e., whether they are coprime). The Euclidean algorithm makes this possible at web
  scale.
