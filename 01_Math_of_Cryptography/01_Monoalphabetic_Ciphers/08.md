Two integers are **coprime** (also known as **relatively prime** or **mutually prime**) if their **Greatest Common Divisor
(GCD) is exactly 1**.

In plain English, this means they share **no common factors** other than the number 1. Even if the numbers themselves are not
prime, they can still be coprime to each other.

---

### 1. Examples of Coprime Numbers

- **Example 1: 8 and 15**
- Divisors of 8: $1, 2, 4, 8$
- Divisors of 15: $1, 3, 5, 15$
- The only shared divisor is **1**. Therefore, $\text{GCD}(8, 15) = 1$, making them **coprime**. _(Notice neither 8 nor 15 is
  a prime number on its own!)_

- **Example 2: 14 and 25**
- Divisors of 14: $1, 2, 7, 14$
- Divisors of 25: $1, 5, 25$
- Shared divisor: **1**. They are **coprime**.

- **Non-Example: 12 and 18**
- Divisors of 12: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$
- Divisors of 18: $1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18$
- They share 1, 2, 3, and 6. Because their GCD is **6** (not 1), they are **not coprime**.

---

### 2. Quick Rules to Remember

- **Consecutive integers** are always coprime (e.g., 99 and 100).
- **Any prime number** is coprime with any number smaller than it that it doesn't divide evenly into (e.g., 7 is coprime with
  8, 9, 10, 11, etc.).

---

### 3. Why Coprimality Matters in Cryptography

As you touched on earlier when looking at modular inverses and Bézout's identity, coprimality is the gatekeeper for division
in modular arithmetic:

- You can only find a **modular multiplicative inverse** for a number $a$ modulo $n$ if $a$ and $n$ are **coprime**
  ($\text{GCD}(a, n) = 1$).
- If they share any common factor greater than 1, the modular inverse doesn't exist, and the mathematical operations used to
  decrypt messages in systems like RSA will break down.
