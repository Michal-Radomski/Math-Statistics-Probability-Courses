Modular arithmetic shares many familiar algebraic properties with standard integer arithmetic, which is part of what makes it
so powerful for computer science and cryptography. Because numbers "wrap around" after reaching the modulus $n$, you can
simplify calculations significantly by reducing numbers at any step.

Here are the core properties of modular arithmetic:

---

### 1. Addition and Subtraction Properties

If two numbers have the same remainder when divided by $n$, you can add or subtract them and the congruences hold true.

If $a \equiv b \pmod n$ and $c \equiv d \pmod n$, then:

- **Addition:** $(a + c) \equiv (b + d) \pmod n$
- **Subtraction:** $(a - c) \equiv (b - d) \pmod n$
- **Example ($\pmod 7$):** Since $10 \equiv 3 \pmod 7$ and $11 \equiv 4 \pmod 7$:
- $(10 + 11) = 21$, and $21 \pmod 7 = 0$.
- $(3 + 4) = 7$, and $7 \pmod 7 = 0$. Both yield the same result!

---

### 2. Multiplication Property

You can also multiply numbers under a modulus without losing the congruence relationship.

If $a \equiv b \pmod n$ and $c \equiv d \pmod n$, then:

- **Multiplication:** $(a \times c) \equiv (b \times d) \pmod n$
- **Example ($\pmod 5$):** Since $8 \equiv 3 \pmod 5$ and $7 \equiv 2 \pmod 5$:
- $8 \times 7 = 56$, and $56 \pmod 5 = 1$.
- $3 \times 2 = 6$, and $6 \pmod 5 = 1$.

---

### 3. Intermediate Reduction (The "Reduce Anytime" Rule)

In standard modular arithmetic, you don't have to wait until the very end of a massive calculation to apply the modulo. You
can reduce numbers **at any intermediate step**. This is a lifesaver for handling huge exponents in cryptography.

- **Example:** Compute $17 \times 23 \pmod 5$.
- Instead of multiplying $17 \times 23 = 391$ and then finding $391 \pmod 5$, reduce the inputs first:
- $17 \equiv 2 \pmod 5$
- $23 \equiv 3 \pmod 5$

- Then multiply the reduced values: $2 \times 3 = 6$, and $6 \pmod 5 = 1$.

---

### 4. Exponentiation Property

Powers behave predictably under a modulus as well, allowing you to raise numbers to massive powers while keeping the numbers
small through repeated reduction.

If $a \equiv b \pmod n$, then:

- **Exponents:** $a^k \equiv b^k \pmod n$ (for any positive integer $k$).

---

### 5. A Note on Division (Modular Inverses)

While addition, subtraction, and multiplication work smoothly, **division is tricky**. You cannot always divide freely in
modular arithmetic.

To divide by a number $a$ modulo $n$, you must find its **modular multiplicative inverse**—a number $x$ such that
$(a \times x) \equiv 1 \pmod n$. Such an inverse only exists if $a$ and $n$ are **coprime** (their greatest common divisor is
1).
