A **General Substitution Cipher** (often called a **simple substitution cipher** or **monoalphabetic substitution cipher**)
is a classic encryption technique where every letter in the plaintext is systematically replaced by another letter or symbol
based on a fixed, mixed-up alphabet key.

Unlike the Caesar or Affine ciphers, which rely on a mathematical formula, a general substitution cipher uses a completely
arbitrary permutation of the 26 letters of the alphabet.

---

## How It Works

To encrypt a message, you create a randomized "substitution alphabet" that maps directly to the standard alphabet.

- **Plaintext alphabet:** `a b c d e f g h i j k l m n o p q r s t u v w x y z`
- **Ciphertext alphabet:** `Q W E R T Y U I O P A S D F G H J K L Z X C V B N M` _(an example random shuffle)_

Using this key:

- Every **'a'** becomes **'Q'**
- Every **'b'** becomes **'W'**
- Every **'c'** becomes **'E'**

If you want to encrypt the word **"CAT"**:

- **C** $\rightarrow$ **E**
- **A** $\rightarrow$ **Q**
- **T** $\rightarrow$ **Z**
- Result: **EQZ**

---

## Massive Key Space

At first glance, this cipher seems remarkably secure because of the sheer number of possible keys. The number of unique ways
to arrange 26 letters is $26!$ (26 factorial):

$$26! \approx 4 \times 10^{26} \text{ (or 400 septillion possible keys)}$$

If a supercomputer could test one billion keys every second, it would still take millions of years to check every single
combination using a brute-force attack.

---

## Why It Can Be Broken Easily

Despite having more keys than atoms in our solar system, the general substitution cipher is **trivially easy to break** using
**frequency analysis**.

1. **Preserving Patterns:** Monoalphabetic ciphers do not hide the underlying statistical structure of a language.
2. **Letter Frequencies:** In English, the letter **'E'** is the most common, followed by **'T'**, **'A'**, **'O'**, **'I'**,
   and **'N'**. Common words like _the_, _and_, and _is_ appear repeatedly.
3. **Pattern Matching:** By analyzing the frequency of letters, common two-letter words (digraphs), and three-letter words
   (trigraphs) in the ciphertext, an analyst can quickly deduce the substitution mapping without testing every key.
