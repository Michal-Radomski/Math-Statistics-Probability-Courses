**Modular arithmetic**—often called **"clock arithmetic"**—is a system of arithmetic for integers where numbers wrap around
after reaching a certain value, known as the **modulus**.

If you have ever looked at a clock, you already use modular arithmetic every day.

---

### 1. The Clock Analogy (Modulo 12)

Imagine a standard 12-hour clock. If it is currently 9:00 and you wait 4 hours, what time is it?

- In normal math: $9 + 4 = 13$.
- On a clock: After 12, the numbers wrap back around to 1. So, $9 + 4 = 1$.

In math terms, we say that $13 \pmod{12} = 1$. The **modulo** (abbreviated as $\text{mod}$) represents the remainder left
over after division.

---

### 2. How to Calculate Modulo

To find $a \pmod n$ (read as "$a$ modulo $n$"), you divide $a$ by $n$ and look at the **remainder**:

- **Example 1:** $17 \pmod 5$
- Divide 17 by 5: $17 \div 5 = 3$ with a **remainder of 2** (since $3 \times 5 = 15$, and $17 - 15 = 2$).
- Therefore, $17 \pmod 5 = 2$.

- **Example 2:** $24 \pmod 6$
- Divide 24 by 6: $24 \div 6 = 4$ with a **remainder of 0** (since 6 goes into 24 evenly).
- Therefore, $24 \pmod 6 = 0$.

---

### 3. Congruence

In modular arithmetic, we use the congruence symbol ($\equiv$) instead of an equals sign when two numbers leave the same
remainder under a given modulus.

For example, $14 \equiv 2 \pmod{6}$ because both 14 and 2 leave a remainder of 2 when divided by 6:

- $14 \div 6 = 2$ remainder $2$
- $2 \div 6 = 0$ remainder $2$

---

### 4. Why Does It Matter?

While it sounds simple, modular arithmetic is the bedrock of modern digital security:

- **Cryptography:** Modern encryption algorithms (like RSA) rely on operations with enormous prime numbers and modular
  arithmetic. It is easy to multiply two giant numbers together, but working backward (finding the original numbers from the
  remainder) is computationally nearly impossible.
- **Computer Science:** Used extensively in hashing algorithms, pseudo-random number generators, and array indexing.
