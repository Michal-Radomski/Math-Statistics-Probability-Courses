**Bézout's identity** (sometimes called Bézout's lemma) is a fundamental theorem in number theory that builds directly on the
Euclidean algorithm.

It states that if two numbers $a$ and $b$ have a greatest common divisor $d = \text{GCD}(a, b)$, then you can always find a
linear combination of those two numbers that equals their GCD.

Mathematically, it looks like this:

$$ax + by = d$$

_(Where $x$ and $y$ are whole integers, which can be positive, negative, or zero)._

---

### 1. A Simple Example

Let's take our numbers from the Euclidean algorithm example earlier: **252** and **105**.

- We found that $\text{GCD}(252, 105) = 21$.

Bézout's identity guarantees that we can find integers $x$ and $y$ such that:

$$252x + 105y = 21$$

If we solve for $x$ and $y$, we find:

- $252(3) + 105(-7) = 756 - 735 = 21$
- Here, $x = 3$ and $y = -7$.

---

### 2. Why Is Bézout's Identity Crucial for Cryptography?

While the standard Euclidean algorithm just gives you the GCD, **Bézout's identity is the key to finding modular
multiplicative inverses.**

As you saw earlier, to do division in modular arithmetic (which modern public-key cryptography like RSA relies on heavily),
you need to find the inverse of a number $a$ modulo $n$. That inverse only exists if $a$ and $n$ are coprime, meaning:

$$\text{GCD}(a, n) = 1$$

When $\text{GCD}(a, n) = 1$, Bézout's identity tells us that:

$$ax + ny = 1$$

If you look at that equation modulo $n$ (where multiples of $n$ drop to 0):

$$ax \equiv 1 \pmod n$$

This means **$x$ is the modular multiplicative inverse of $a$!**

To actually find $x$ and $y$ for massive numbers, cryptographers use a slight upgrade to the Euclidean algorithm called the
**Extended Euclidean Algorithm**.
