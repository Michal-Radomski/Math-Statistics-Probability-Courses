The **Extended Euclidean Algorithm** is an extension of the classic Euclidean algorithm used to find the greatest common
divisor (GCD) of two integers. Not only does it compute $\gcd(a, b)$, but it also finds two integers $x$ and $y$ (called
**Bézout coefficients**) that satisfy **Bézout's identity**:

$$a \cdot x + b \cdot y = \gcd(a, b)$$

This algorithm is essential in cryptography—specifically for finding **modular multiplicative inverses** (such as finding
$a^{-1} \pmod{26}$ in the Affine Cipher).

---

## Why is it Important?

To decrypt an Affine Cipher, you need to solve for $a^{-1}$ where $(a \cdot a^{-1}) \equiv 1 \pmod{26}$. This is equivalent
to solving the linear Diophantine equation:

$$a \cdot x + 26 \cdot y = 1$$

The Extended Euclidean algorithm solves this exact equation efficiently.

---

## How It Works

The algorithm builds on the standard Euclidean algorithm, which uses repeated division with remainder:

1. $a = q_1 \cdot b + r_1$
2. $b = q_2 \cdot r_1 + r_2$
3. $r_1 = q_3 \cdot r_2 + r_3$ ...and so on until the remainder is $0$.

The extended version tracks how the remainders can be expressed as linear combinations of $a$ and $b$ at each step, working
backward (back-substitution) to find the final coefficients $x$ and $y$.

---

## Step-by-Step Example

Let's find the modular inverse of $5 \pmod{26}$ by solving $5x + 26y = \gcd(5, 26) = 1$.

### Step 1: Forward Pass (Standard Euclidean Division)

- $26 = 5 \cdot 5 + 1$ (Remainder $1$)
- $5 = 5 \cdot 1 + 0$ (Remainder $0$, so $\gcd(26, 5) = 1$)

### Step 2: Backward Pass (Expressing the GCD)

Rearrange the first equation to isolate the remainder ($1$):

$$1 = 26 - (5 \cdot 5)$$

Rewrite this in the form $5x + 26y = 1$:

$$5(-5) + 26(1) = 1$$

### Step 3: Extracting the Modular Inverse

From the equation above, our coefficient for $5$ is $-5$. To make it a positive modular inverse modulo 26:

$$(-5 \pmod{26} + 26) = 21$$

Thus, $5^{-1} \equiv 21 \pmod{26}$ (since $5 \times 21 = 105$, and $105 \pmod{26} = 1$).
