A **common divisor** (also called a common factor) is a whole number that divides evenly into two or more given integers
without leaving a remainder.

In number theory and cryptography, understanding common divisors—especially the **Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)**—is
essential for working with fractions, simplifying ratios, and determining if numbers are coprime.

---

### 1. What is a Common Divisor?

To find the common divisors of two numbers, you first list all the factors (divisors) of each number and then find which ones
they share.

- **Example:** Find the common divisors of **12** and **18**.
- **Divisors of 12** (numbers that divide 12 evenly): $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$
- **Divisors of 18** (numbers that divide 18 evenly): $1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18$
- **Shared (Common) Divisors:** $1, 2, 3,$ and $6$.

---

### 2. The Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)

Among all the common divisors shared by a set of numbers, the **Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)**—sometimes called the Highest
Common Factor (HCF)—is the largest one.

- Using our example above:
- Common divisors of 12 and 18: $\{1, 2, 3, 6\}$
- **$\text{GCD}(12, 18) = 6$**

---

### 3. What Does GCD Mean for Cryptography? (Coprime Numbers)

As you saw when discussing modular inverses, the GCD plays a critical role in whether you can perform division in modular
arithmetic:

- **Coprime (Relatively Prime):** If the greatest common divisor of two numbers is **1**, we say they are **coprime** (or
  relatively prime).
- Example: $\text{GCD}(8, 15) = 1$ (the only shared divisor is 1).

- **Why it matters:** In cryptographic systems like RSA, a modular inverse (which acts like division) _only_ exists for a
  number $a$ modulo $n$ if $a$ and $n$ are coprime ($\text{GCD}(a, n) = 1$). If their GCD is anything greater than 1,
  division breaks down.
