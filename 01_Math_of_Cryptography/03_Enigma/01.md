The **Enigma machine** was an electromechanical rotor cipher device used by the German military and government agencies
before and during World War II to encrypt and decrypt secret communications. For years, the Axis powers believed Enigma
messages were completely unbreakable, making it one of the most famous cryptographic systems in history.

---

### How It Works

At its core, the Enigma machine looks like a typewriter combined with a complex maze of electrical wiring.

1. **The Keyboard:** When an operator pressed a letter key on the keyboard, an electrical circuit was completed.
2. **The Plugboard (Steckerbrett):** Before the current even entered the internal ciphers, it passed through a plugboard at
   the front of the machine, which swapped pairs of letters (e.g., 'A' might be wired to connect with 'G').
3. **The Rotors (Walzen):** The current then passed through three to four rotating wheels (rotors) arranged in a line. Each
   rotor had a different internal wiring pattern that scrambled the letters. Crucially, **every time a key was pressed, the
   rightmost rotor clicked and rotated**, changing the electrical pathway.
4. **The Reflector (Umkehrwalze):** After passing through the rotors, the current hit a reflector at the end, which sent the
   signal back _through_ the rotors a second time by a different path.
5. **The Lampboard:** Finally, a lightbulb lit up on a board above the keyboard, showing the encrypted ciphertext letter.

Because the rotors shifted with every single keystroke, pressing the letter **'E'** five times in a row would result in five
completely different ciphertext letters (for example: **Q, H, X, B, M**). This made Enigma an immensely complex
polyalphabetic substitution cipher.

---

### The Daily Settings

To decrypt an Enigma message, the receiving operator's machine had to be set up in the exact same configuration as the
sender's machine for that day. These settings included:

- **Rotor Order:** Which rotors were placed in positions 1, 2, and 3.
- **Ring Settings:** The internal starting positions of the alphabets on each rotor.
- **Plugboard Connections:** Which letters were swapped with cables on the front board.
- **Initial Rotor Positions:** The starting letters visible to the operator (e.g., `WDL`).

German military procedures required these settings to be changed daily, distributing codebooks to ensure all units matched.

---

### How It Was Cracked

Because the German military changed the Enigma settings every day, brute-forcing all possibilities by hand was impossible.
However, Allied codebreakers—most famously **Alan Turing** and his team at Bletchley Park in the UK—managed to break the
system by exploiting human error, flaws in German procedures, and early mechanical computers called **Bombes**.

Breaking the Enigma code provided the Allies with vital intelligence (codename _Ultra_), significantly shortening World War
II and saving millions of lives.
