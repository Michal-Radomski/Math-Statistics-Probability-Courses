The efficiency of these codebreaking machines evolved dramatically throughout World War II, moving from slow, manual-heavy
setups to industrial-scale processing.

---

### 1. The Polish _Bomba_: Efficient, Until the Rules Changed

- **Speed:** In the mid-1930s, when the German Enigma was simpler (using only 3 rotors and no plugboard complexity), the
  Polish _Bomba_ was remarkably efficient. Marian Rejewski’s card catalog and the _Bomba_ could find daily settings in
  **under two hours**.
- **The Bottleneck:** When Germany drastically increased security by adding more possible rotors (choosing 3 out of 5) and
  introducing the **plugboard** (which scrambled letters before and after they hit the rotors), the number of possible
  combinations skyrocketed into the billions. The Polish Bomba could no longer keep up on its own.

---

### 2. The British Turing-Welchman _Bombe_: Industrial-Scale Speed

Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman completely re-engineered the concept to handle these massive new variables. Their efficiency
came down to smart logic and brute-force automation working together.

- **Test Time per Rotor Order:** A single Bombe could test all **17,576 possible starting positions**
  ($26 \times 26 \times 26$) for a given rotor order in roughly **10 to 20 minutes**.
- **The Magic of Welchman's "Diagonal Board":** Turing's original design suffered from too many "false stops"—moments where
  the machine halted because it found a coincidence, only for operators to discover it was a dead end. Gordon Welchman solved
  this by adding the **diagonal board**, a wiring matrix that allowed the machine to cross-check multiple logical constraints
  simultaneously. This slashed false stops and cut down processing time drastically.
- **Mass Production:** Bletchley Park didn't rely on just one machine. By the end of the war, they built over **200 Bombes**.
  Operated 24/7 by members of the Women's Royal Naval Service (WRNS), these machines ran parallel tests around the clock.

---

### 3. Real-World Efficiency: Beating the Clock

The ultimate measure of the Bombe’s efficiency wasn't just raw computing speed—it was whether they could decrypt messages
**before the information expired**.

Because the German military changed their Enigma keys **every single day** (and sometimes more often for U-boats), Bletchley
Park was in a constant race against time. Once a cryptanalyst found a good "crib" (a guessed phrase in a morning message),
the Bombes could usually identify the day's settings within hours.

This allowed Allied command centers to intercept, decrypt, and act on critical U-boat movements and supply orders while they
were still happening.
