The **Student's t-distribution** (often just called the **t-distribution**) is a close cousin of the normal distribution, but
it is specifically designed for situations where your sample size is small or you don't know the true population standard
deviation.

While the Central Limit Theorem tells us that sample means form a normal distribution when samples are large ($n \ge 30$),
what happens when you only have data from 10 or 15 people? That is where the t-distribution saves the day.

---

### Why Do We Need It?

When you have a small sample, you have less information about the wider world. Because of this uncertainty, your estimates
are more likely to bounce around.

If you use a standard normal ($z$) distribution with small samples, you risk being overly confident and underestimating your
margins of error. The t-distribution corrects for this by having **fatter tails**. Those higher tails mean extreme values are
more likely, which widens your confidence intervals and makes your hypothesis tests more honest.

---

### Key Characteristics of the T-Distribution

1. **Bell-Shaped and Symmetrical:** Just like the normal distribution, it is centered at zero and symmetrical.
2. **Controlled by Degrees of Freedom ($df$):** The shape of the t-distribution depends on your sample size through the
   **degrees of freedom** (usually calculated as $n - 1$).

- With a very small sample, the tails are quite fat and the peak is shorter.
- As your sample size ($n$) grows larger, the degrees of freedom increase, and the t-distribution **morphs directly into the
  standard normal distribution**.

---

### When to Use a Z-Score vs. a T-Score

| Condition                                                                           | Use This Score | Why?                                                                                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Large sample ($n \ge 30$) OR population standard deviation ($\sigma$) is known**  | **Z-score**    | You have enough data or certainty to rely on the standard normal distribution.                                              |
| **Small sample ($n < 30$) AND population standard deviation ($\sigma$) is unknown** | **T-score**    | You must use the sample standard deviation ($s$), which adds uncertainty, requiring the fatter tails of the t-distribution. |
