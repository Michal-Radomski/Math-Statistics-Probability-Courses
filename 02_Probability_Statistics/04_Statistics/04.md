**Hypothesis testing** is a formal, scientific way to use sample data to test a claim or make a decision about a population.

If sampling, the Central Limit Theorem, and standard error are the tools in your statistical toolbox, hypothesis testing is
how you actually put them to work to answer questions like: _Does this new medicine actually work?_ or _Did this website
redesign increase sales?_

Think of hypothesis testing like a **courtroom trial**: the defendant is assumed innocent until proven guilty beyond a
reasonable doubt.

---

### The Two Hypotheses

Every test starts by setting up two competing statements:

1. **The Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** The default assumption that _nothing is happening_—there is no effect, no difference, or
   no relationship (e.g., "The new study method has no effect on test scores").
2. **The Alternative Hypothesis ($H_a$ or $H_1$):** What the researcher actually suspects or wants to prove—that _there is an
   effect or a difference_ (e.g., "The new study method improves test scores").

Your goal is to look at your sample data and see if there is enough evidence to **reject the null hypothesis** in favor of
the alternative.

---

### How the Process Works

1. **Set a Significance Level ($\alpha$):** Researchers usually pick a threshold like $\alpha = 0.05$ (5%). This represents
   how willing you are to make a mistake by rejecting the null hypothesis when it was actually true.
2. **Calculate a Test Statistic:** Using your sample data and standard error, you calculate a score (like a $z$-score or
   $t$-score) that measures how far your sample result is from what the null hypothesis predicted.
3. **Find the P-Value:** The **p-value** tells you the probability of getting your sample results (or more extreme) purely by
   chance, assuming the null hypothesis is true.
4. **Make Your Decision:**

- If the p-value is **low** (typically less than 0.05), your results are statistically significant. You **reject $H_0$**.
- If the p-value is **high**, you **fail to reject $H_0$** (meaning there isn't enough evidence to claim an effect).
