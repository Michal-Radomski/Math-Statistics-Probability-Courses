In statistics, **sampling** is the process of selecting a subset of individuals or items from a larger group (the
**population**) to gather data and make inferences about the whole group.

Because it is usually too expensive, time-consuming, or physically impossible to study every single member of a population
(such as every teenager in a country or every fish in an ocean), researchers rely on well-chosen samples.

---

### Key Terms to Know

- **Population:** The entire group of people, events, or things you want to draw conclusions about (e.g., all high school
  students in a state).
- **Sample:** The smaller, representative group actually surveyed or measured.
- **Parameter:** A numerical characteristic describing a _population_ (often unknown and estimated).
- **Statistic:** A numerical characteristic calculated from a _sample_ used to estimate the population parameter.

---

### Common Sampling Methods

How you choose your sample matters immensely. If your sample isn't representative, your conclusions will be biased or
incorrect. Here are the most common methods:

1. **Simple Random Sampling:** Every member of the population has an exact, equal chance of being selected (like drawing
   names out of a hat or using a random number generator).
2. **Stratified Sampling:** The population is divided into meaningful subgroups (called _strata_), such as grade levels or
   age groups, and a random sample is taken from each subgroup to ensure fair representation.
3. **Systematic Sampling:** You choose a starting point at random and then pick every $n$-th person on a list (e.g., every
   10th student walking through the door).
4. **Convenience Sampling:** Choosing individuals who are easiest to reach (e.g., surveying your friends in the cafeteria).
   While easy, this method introduces high **sampling bias** and rarely represents the wider population accurately.
