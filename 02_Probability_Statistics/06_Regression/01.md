In statistics, **regression** is a set of statistical methods used to estimate the relationship between a **dependent
variable** (what you want to predict or explain) and one or more **independent variables** (the predictors or features).

If correlation tells you _if_ two things are related, regression gives you the mathematical equation to actually _predict_
one from the other. It’s the engine behind forecasting, trend analysis, and predictive modeling.

---

### Key Concepts in Regression

1. **Dependent Variable ($y$):** The outcome or target you are trying to predict (e.g., a house's selling price).
2. **Independent Variable ($x$):** The predictor or input factor you use to make that prediction (e.g., the square footage of
   the house).
3. **The Line of Best Fit:** In **simple linear regression**, the model draws a straight line through the data points that
   minimizes the overall distance (the residuals we talked about with MSE and RMSE) between the actual data and the line.

---

### The Simple Linear Regression Equation

You might recognize this from algebra, but statistics puts it to work with a few Greek letters:

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \epsilon$$

- $y$ = Predicted value
- $\beta_0$ = The **y-intercept** (the expected value of $y$ when $x$ is zero)
- $\beta_1$ = The **slope** (how much $y$ changes on average for every one-unit increase in $x$)
- $\epsilon$ = The **error term** (the leftover random noise or variation the model couldn't capture)

---

### Types of Regression

Depending on your data, regression comes in many flavors:

- **Simple Linear Regression:** One predictor variable and one continuous outcome ($y = mx + b$).
- **Multiple Linear Regression:** Multiple predictor variables trying to predict one continuous outcome (e.g., predicting
  house prices using square footage, age of the home, and number of bedrooms).
- **Logistic Regression:** Used when your outcome isn't a continuous number, but a **binary category** (e.g., Yes/No,
  Pass/Fail, Spam/Not Spam).
