In probability and combinatorics, a **permutation** is an arrangement of items in a **specific order**.

The fundamental rule that distinguishes permutations from combinations is that **order matters**. For example, finishing a
race in 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place is a permutation because rearranging the order changes the outcome (Alice then Bob vs. Bob
then Alice).

---

### 1. Permutations of $n$ Items Taken $r$ at a Time (Without Repetition)

When you want to select and arrange $r$ items from a total pool of $n$ distinct items without replacement, the formula is:

$$P(n, r) = \frac{n!}{(n - r)!}$$

- $n$ = total number of items in the set
- $r$ = number of items to choose and arrange
- $!$ = factorial (e.g., $5! = 5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 120$)

**Example:** How many ways can you award 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place prizes to 5 contestants?

$$P(5, 3) = \frac{5!}{(5 - 3)!} = \frac{5!}{2!} = \frac{120}{2} = 60 \text{ ways}$$

---

### 2. Permutations of All Items ($n = r$)

If you are arranging _all_ $n$ items in a set, the formula simplifies because $(n - n)! = 0! = 1$:

$$P(n, n) = n!$$

**Example:** How many different ways can 4 books be arranged on a shelf?

$$P(4, 4) = 4! = 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 24 \text{ ways}$$

---

### 3. Permutations with Repetition

If items can be chosen more than once (repetition is allowed), the number of choices for each of the $r$ positions remains
$n$. The formula is:

$$P_{\text{repetition}} = n^r$$

**Example:** How many 4-digit PIN codes are possible if each digit can be any number from 0 to 9 ($n = 10$, $r = 4$)?

$$10^4 = 10,000 \text{ possibilities}$$
