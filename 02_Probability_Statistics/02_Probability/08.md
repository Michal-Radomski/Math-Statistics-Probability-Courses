Words like **centile, decile, quartile, and quantile** belong to a family of statistical terms known collectively as
**quantiles**. They are used to divide a dataset or probability distribution into equal-sized subgroups or intervals.

The name of each term usually comes from the number of parts it divides the data into. Here is a breakdown of these terms,
ordered from the smallest number of divisions to the largest:

---

### 1. The Umbrella Term

- **Quantile:** The general, overarching term for any point that divides a dataset into continuous intervals with equal
  probabilities. Every term below is a specific type of quantile.

---

### 2. Specific Quantile Terms (by Number of Divisions)

| Term                     | Number of Divisions | Number of Cut Points              | Common Use Cases                                                                                                       |
| ------------------------ | ------------------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tertile**              | 3 equal parts       | 2 cut points                      | Dividing groups into thirds (e.g., low, medium, high income or performance).                                           |
| **Quartile**             | 4 equal parts       | 3 cut points ($Q_1, Q_2, Q_3$)    | Analyzing test scores, financial metrics, and box plots ($Q_2$ is the median).                                         |
| **Quintile**             | 5 equal parts       | 4 cut points                      | Widely used in economics to study income or wealth distribution across a population (e.g., "the top income quintile"). |
| **Sextile**              | 6 equal parts       | 5 cut points                      | Less common, used when a dataset needs to be split into six equal segments.                                            |
| **Octile**               | 8 equal parts       | 7 cut points                      | Used in specialized statistical distributions divided into eighths.                                                    |
| **Decile**               | 10 equal parts      | 9 cut points ($D_1$ to $D_9$)     | Ranking performance or scores from 1 to 10 (e.g., credit scores, educational rankings).                                |
| **Percentile (Centile)** | 100 equal parts     | 99 cut points ($P_1$ to $P_{99}$) | Extremely common for standardized testing (e.g., SAT scores) and pediatric health charts (baby's weight/height).       |

---

### Key Takeaway

The formulaic pattern is simple: **$N$-tile** divides the data into **$N$ equal parts** using **$N-1$ partition points**. For
example, a decile splits data into 10 parts using 9 cut points, and a percentile splits data into 100 parts using 99 cut
points.
