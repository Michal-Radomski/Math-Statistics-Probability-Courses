A **Chi-Square Analysis** is a statistical test used to examine differences between categorical variables. It helps
researchers determine whether the data they observe in a study significantly deviates from what they would expect to find by
chance.

---

### The Two Main Types of Chi-Square Tests

1. **Chi-Square Goodness of Fit Test**

- **Purpose:** Tests whether a single categorical variable matches an expected population distribution.
- **Example:** Checking if a die is fair by rolling it 60 times and comparing the frequency of each number to an expected 10
  rolls per number.

2. **Chi-Square Test of Independence**

- **Purpose:** Tests whether two categorical variables are related or independent of each other.
- **Example:** Investigating whether favorite genre of music (Rock, Pop, Classical) is dependent on age group (Teens, Adults,
  Seniors).

---

### The Chi-Square Formula

The core of the test relies on comparing **Observed ($O$)** frequencies against **Expected ($E$)** frequencies using this
formula:

$$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}$$

- **$O$**: The actual frequency count observed in your data.
- **$E$**: The theoretical frequency count you would expect if there were no association (the null hypothesis is true).
- **$\sum$**: The sum across all categories or table cells.

### How It Works in Practice

1. **State Hypotheses:**

- Null Hypothesis ($H_0$): There is no association between the variables (they are independent).
- Alternative Hypothesis ($H_a$): There is an association between the variables.

2. **Calculate Expected Values:** Determine what the cell counts would look like under the null hypothesis.
3. **Compute the Chi-Square Statistic:** Plug your observed and expected values into the formula.
4. **Compare to a Critical Value / $p$-value:** Determine if the resulting $\chi^2$ value is large enough to reject the null
   hypothesis based on your degrees of freedom and chosen significance level (usually $\alpha = 0.05$).
