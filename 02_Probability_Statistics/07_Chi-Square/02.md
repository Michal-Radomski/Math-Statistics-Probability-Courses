The **chi-squared distribution** (often written as the $\chi^2$ distribution) is a fundamental continuous probability
distribution in statistics. It is most commonly used in hypothesis testing, such as in goodness-of-fit tests and tests of
independence in contingency tables.

---

### Definition and Construction

Formally, if $Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_k$ are $k$ independent, standard normal random variables (each with mean $0$ and variance
$1$), then the sum of their squared values follows a chi-squared distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom:

$$\chi^2 = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 + \dots + Z_k^2$$

The parameter **$k$** (degrees of freedom) defines the specific shape of the distribution.

---

### Key Properties

- **Support:** The chi-squared distribution is defined only for non-negative values ($x \ge 0$), because it is formed by
  summing squared numbers.
- **Mean:** Equal to the degrees of freedom, $\mu = k$.
- **Variance:** Equal to twice the degrees of freedom, $\sigma^2 = 2k$.
- **Shape:**
- For small values of $k$ (e.g., $k \le 2$), the distribution is heavily **right-skewed** (asymmetric, with a long tail
  stretching to the right).
- As $k$ increases, the distribution becomes more bell-shaped and symmetric. According to the Central Limit Theorem, as $k$
  approaches infinity, the chi-squared distribution approximates a normal distribution.

---

### Common Applications

1. **Chi-Squared Goodness-of-Fit Test:** Determines whether categorical data matches an expected theoretical distribution
   (e.g., testing whether a die is fair).
2. **Chi-Squared Test of Independence:** Examines whether two categorical variables are related or independent (e.g., testing
   if voting preference is independent of age group).
3. **Variance Estimation:** Used in inferential statistics to construct confidence intervals for the population variance of a
   normally distributed population.
