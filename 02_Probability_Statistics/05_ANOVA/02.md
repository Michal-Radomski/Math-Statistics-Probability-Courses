The **F-distribution** (sometimes called the Fisher-Snedecor distribution) is a continuous probability distribution that is
heavily used in inferential statistics—most notably in **ANOVA** and regression analysis.

If you remember from our look at ANOVA, we calculated an **F-statistic** (a ratio comparing variance _between_ groups to
variance _within_ groups). The F-distribution is the theoretical probability distribution that tells us how that F-statistic
behaves purely by chance under the null hypothesis.

---

### Key Characteristics of the F-Distribution

Unlike the normal distribution or Student's t-distribution (which are symmetrical and centered around zero), the
F-distribution has some unique traits:

1. **Always Non-Negative ($F \ge 0$):** Because it is calculated as a ratio of variances (and variance squared can never be
   negative), an F-value can never be less than zero.
2. **Right-Skewed:** The curve has a peak near the left and a long tail stretching out to the right. As the values get
   larger, they become increasingly rare.
3. **Controlled by _Two_ Degrees of Freedom:** While the t-distribution relies on a single degree of freedom ($n - 1$), the
   F-distribution requires **two**:

- **Numerator Degrees of Freedom ($df_1$):** Based on the number of groups being compared (or parameters in a model).
- **Denominator Degrees of Freedom ($df_2$):** Based on the total sample size minus the number of groups.

---

### How It Works in Hypothesis Testing

When you run an ANOVA or test a regression model, you get an actual $F$-score from your data. You then compare that score to
the theoretical F-distribution based on your $df_1$ and $df_2$.

- If your calculated $F$-score lands way out in the far right tail of the distribution, it means the variance between your
  groups is far too large to be a coincidence.
- This yields a **low p-value**, allowing you to reject the null hypothesis and conclude that at least some of your groups
  are genuinely different.
