When building predictive models in statistics and machine learning (like linear regression), you need a way to measure how
far off your model's predictions are from reality.

The three most common metrics used to evaluate prediction error are **MAE**, **MSE**, and **RMSE**. They all look at the
**residuals** (the differences between actual values and predicted values), but they treat those errors differently.

---

### 1. MAE (Mean Absolute Error)

MAE is the average of the _absolute_ differences between predictions and actual values.

- **How it's calculated:** You take every error, turn it into a positive number (by taking its absolute value), add them all
  up, and divide by the number of data points ($n$).
- **Formula:**

$$MAE = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \vert{}y_i - \hat{y}_i\vert{}$$

- **What it means intuitively:** MAE gives you the average "magnitude" of your errors. If you are predicting house prices and
  your MAE is $\$10,000$, it means your model is off by $\$10,000$ on average.
- **Key trait:** It treats all errors equally. A miss of $\$50,000$ is weighted exactly five times as much as a miss of
  $\$10,000$.

---

### 2. MSE (Mean Squared Error)

MSE is the average of the _squared_ differences between predictions and actual values.

- **How it's calculated:** You take every error, square it (making negative errors positive and blowing larger errors way
  up), add them up, and divide by $n$.
- **Formula:**

$$MSE = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

- **What it means intuitively:** Because the errors are squared, **larger errors are heavily penalized**. If you are off by a
  little bit, the squared error is small. But if you are off by a lot, the squared error explodes.
- **Key trait:** Statisticians love MSE mathematically because squaring makes the error function smooth and easy to
  differentiate, which is great for optimization algorithms. However, its units are squared (e.g., "dollars squared"), which
  makes it hard to interpret in the real world.

---

### 3. RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error)

RMSE is simply the square root of the MSE. It brings the error metric back down to the original units of your data.

- **How it's calculated:** Take the MSE and calculate its square root.
- **Formula:**

$$RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}$$

- **What it means intuitively:** Like MSE, RMSE punishes large errors harshly because of the squaring step. But because we
  take the square root at the end, the final number is back in normal units (like actual dollars or temperature degrees).

---

### Summary Comparison

| Metric   | Full Name               | Sensitivity to Outliers                 | Units                 | Best Used When...                                                                                                                      |
| -------- | ----------------------- | --------------------------------------- | --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **MAE**  | Mean Absolute Error     | **Low** (treats all errors linearly)    | Same as original data | You want an intuitive error measure and want to avoid letting a few massive outliers skew your evaluation.                             |
| **MSE**  | Mean Squared Error      | **High** (heavily penalizes big errors) | Squared units         | You are optimizing mathematical models where penalizing large mistakes is crucial.                                                     |
| **RMSE** | Root Mean Squared Error | **High** (heavily penalizes big errors) | Same as original data | You want the mathematical benefits of squaring large errors, but need the final output to be easily interpretable in real-world units. |
