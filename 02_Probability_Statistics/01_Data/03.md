When analyzing a set of quantitative data, two key types of measurements help describe it: **measures of central tendency**
and **measures of dispersion**. Together, they give a complete picture of where the data is centered and how spread out it
is.

---

### Measures of Central Tendency

Central tendency measures point toward the "center" or typical value of a dataset. The three main ones are:

- **Mean:** The arithmetic average, calculated by adding all the numbers together and dividing by the total count.
- **Median:** The exact middle value when all numbers are arranged in order from least to greatest. It is less affected by
  extreme outliers than the mean.
- **Mode:** The value that appears most frequently in the dataset.

---

### Measures of Dispersion (Spread)

Dispersion measures how far apart or spread out the data points are from each other and from the center. Two datasets can
have the exact same average but completely different spreads. The main measures include:

- **Range:** The difference between the highest and lowest values in the dataset.
- **Standard Deviation:** A measure of how much, on average, data points deviate from the mean. A low standard deviation
  means data points cluster closely around the mean, while a high standard deviation means they are widely spread out.
- **Variance:** The square of the standard deviation, representing the average squared distance from the mean.
- **Interquartile Range (IQR):** The range containing the middle 50% of the data (the difference between the 75th and 25th
  percentiles), which helps ignore extreme outliers.

---

### Why Both Matter Together

Relying on central tendency alone can be misleading. For example, two different classrooms might both have an average test
score of 75%. However, in the first classroom, every student scored between 70% and 80%. In the second classroom, half the
students failed and the other half got perfect 100s. Measures of dispersion reveal that vital difference.
