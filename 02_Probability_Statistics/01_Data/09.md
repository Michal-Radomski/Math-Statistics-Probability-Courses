The **Pearson Correlation Coefficient** (commonly denoted as $r$ for a sample or $\rho$ for a population) is a statistical
measure that calculates the strength and direction of the **linear relationship** between two continuous variables.

Building directly on covariance, the Pearson coefficient overcomes covariance's limitation with units by standardizing the
measure into a clean, unitless scale.

---

### Key Characteristics of Pearson's $r$

The resulting value of the Pearson correlation coefficient always falls strictly between **$-1$ and $+1$**:

- **$r = +1$:** A **perfect positive linear relationship**. As one variable increases, the other increases proportionally in
  a straight line.
- **$r = -1$:** A **perfect negative linear relationship**. As one variable increases, the other decreases proportionally in
  a straight line.
- **$r = 0$:** **No linear relationship**. The variables do not move together in a linear pattern (though a non-linear or
  curved relationship might still exist).

### Interpreting the Strength of $r$

While exact thresholds can vary depending on the field of study, general guidelines for interpreting the absolute value of
$r$ include:

- **$0.00$ to $0.19$:** Very weak or no relationship
- **$0.20$ to $0.39$:** Weak relationship
- **$0.40$ to $0.59$:** Moderate relationship
- **$0.60$ to $0.79$:** Strong relationship
- **$0.80$ to $1.00$:** Very strong relationship

---

### The Formula

Mathematically, the Pearson correlation coefficient divides the covariance of the two variables by the product of their
standard deviations:

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}$$

Where:

- $n$ is the sample size.
- $x_i$ and $y_i$ are the individual sample points.
- $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ are the sample means.

### Assumptions and Limitations

To use the Pearson correlation coefficient reliably, the data generally needs to meet a few key assumptions:

1. **Continuous Data:** Both variables should be measured on an interval or ratio scale.
2. **Linearity:** The relationship between the variables must be linear; Pearson will miss strong _curved_ or non-linear
   relationships.
3. **Normality:** The variables should ideally follow a roughly normal distribution.
4. **Outlier Sensitivity:** Like the mean and standard deviation, Pearson's $r$ can be heavily skewed or distorted by extreme
   outliers.
