**Bivariate data** and **covariance** are fundamental concepts in statistics used to analyze the relationship between two
different variables.

---

### Bivariate Data

Bivariate data involves the observation or measurement of **two distinct variables** for each individual item or subject in a
dataset. While univariate data looks at a single characteristic (like the height of students in a class), bivariate data
looks at two characteristics simultaneously (like both the height _and_ the weight of each student).

- **Purpose:** The main goal of collecting bivariate data is to see if there is a relationship, pattern, or correlation
  between the two variables.
- **Representation:** Data is typically represented as pairs of values $(x, y)$, where $x$ is the independent (or
  explanatory) variable and $y$ is the dependent (or response) variable.
- **Visualization:** Bivariate data is most commonly visualized using a **scatterplot**, where each data point is plotted on
  a 2D grid based on its $x$ and $y$ values.
- **Examples:**
- Study hours ($x$) vs. Exam scores ($y$)
- Temperature ($x$) vs. Ice cream sales ($y$)
- Height ($x$) vs. Weight ($y$)

---

### Covariance

**Covariance** is a numerical measure that indicates the direction of the linear relationship between two variables. In
simpler terms, it tells you whether two variables tend to move together or in opposite directions.

- **Positive Covariance:** Indicates that as one variable increases, the other variable tends to also increase. (e.g., more
  study hours generally lead to higher exam scores).
- **Negative Covariance:** Indicates that as one variable increases, the other variable tends to decrease. (e.g., more hours
  spent playing video games right before an exam might correlate with lower scores).
- **Zero (or Near-Zero) Covariance:** Indicates that there is no linear pattern or relationship between the movement of the
  two variables.

#### The Formula

For a sample of $n$ data points, the sample covariance $\text{cov}(x, y)$ is calculated as:

$$\text{cov}(x, y) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{n - 1}$$

Where:

- $x_i$ and $y_i$ are the individual data values.
- $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ are the sample means of the $x$ and $y$ variables.
- $n$ is the total number of data pairs.

#### Limitations of Covariance

While covariance is useful for showing the _direction_ of a relationship, it has a major drawback: **its value is affected by
the units of measurement.**

Because it depends on the scale of the variables, a covariance value of $500$ doesn't automatically mean a "strong"
relationship unless you know the scale of the data. For this reason, statisticians often convert covariance into a
standardized, unitless measure called the **correlation coefficient** (such as Pearson's $r$), which always falls between
$-1$ and $+1$ and clearly shows both the strength and direction of the relationship.
