In statistics and probability, **data** refers to the raw material—observations, measurements, counts, or responses—collected
to be analyzed, interpreted, and used for decision-making.

Data is generally divided into two main categories: **quantitative** (numerical) and **qualitative** (categorical).

---

### 1. Quantitative (Numerical) Data

Quantitative data represents amounts, measurements, or anything that can be counted or measured with numbers. It is further
split into two types:

- **Discrete Data:** Consists of distinct, countable values (usually whole numbers). You can count them individually.
- _Examples:_ The number of students in a classroom, the number of pets you own, or the number of goals scored in a soccer
  match.

- **Continuous Data:** Consists of measurements that can take on any value within a given range, often including decimals and
  fractions. It is measured rather than counted.
- _Examples:_ Height, weight, temperature, or the exact amount of time it takes to run a mile.

### 2. Qualitative (Categorical) Data

Qualitative data describes qualities, characteristics, or categories rather than numbers. It places observations into groups.
It is typically split into:

- **Nominal Data:** Categories that have no inherent order or ranking. They are just labels.
- _Examples:_ Eye color (blue, brown, green), genres of music (rock, pop, jazz), or car brands.

- **Ordinal Data:** Categories that have a meaningful order or ranking, though the exact distance between the ranks isn't
  necessarily measurable.
- _Examples:_ Survey responses (e.g., "Poor, Fair, Good, Excellent"), movie ratings (1 to 5 stars), or finishing places in a
  race (1st, 2nd, 3rd).

---

### How Data Powers Statistics and Probability

- **In Statistics:** Data is the starting point. Statisticians collect samples of data from a larger population, clean it,
  and use tools like graphs, averages, and regression models to uncover patterns, test hypotheses, and draw conclusions.
- **In Probability:** Data acts as the bridge between theory and reality. While theoretical probability calculates what
  _should_ happen in an ideal world (like a 50% chance of heads on a coin flip), empirical probability uses actual collected
  data from experiments to see what _actually_ happened over hundreds of trials.
