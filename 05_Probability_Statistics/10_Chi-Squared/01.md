The **Chi-Squared Distribution** (written as the $\chi^2$ distribution) is a fundamental continuous probability distribution
used extensively in statistics, particularly in hypothesis testing and determining how well data fits a theoretical model.

---

### What is a Chi-Squared Distribution?

Imagine you take several independent random variables that follow a standard normal distribution (mean of 0, variance of 1),
square each of them, and then add them all together. The resulting sum follows a chi-squared distribution.

Mathematically, if $Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_k$ are independent standard normal variables, then the sum of their squares:

$$\chi^2 = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 + \dots + Z_k^2$$

follows a chi-squared distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom.

---

### Key Properties & Shape

- **Degrees of Freedom ($df$ or $k$):** This is the only parameter of the distribution. It represents the number of
  independent variables being summed. As the degrees of freedom increase, the distribution becomes more symmetrical and
  shifts to the right.
- **Always Positive:** Because you are summing _squared_ values, a chi-squared value can never be negative (it starts at 0
  and extends infinitely to the right).
- **Right-Skewed:** For smaller degrees of freedom, the distribution is heavily skewed to the right (tail stretches out). As
  $k$ grows large (usually above 30), it starts to look more like a normal bell curve.

---

### Common Applications

1. **Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test:** Tests whether an observed frequency distribution matches an expected distribution
   (e.g., checking if a dice is fair by rolling it 60 times and comparing outcomes).
2. **Chi-Square Test of Independence:** Tests whether two categorical variables are related or independent (e.g., whether ice
   cream flavor preference is independent of age group).
3. **Variance Estimation:** Used in statistics to construct confidence intervals for the population variance of a normally
   distributed population.
