Building directly on our look at Type I errors (false positives), the flip side of the statistical coin is the **Type II
error** (false negative).

A **Type II error** happens when you **fail to reject a false null hypothesis ($H_0$)**. In plain English: a real effect,
difference, or pattern actually _does_ exist in reality, but your test fails to catch it, leading you to wrongly conclude
that "nothing is happening."

---

### The Classic Analogies

To keep them straight from Type I errors, let's revisit our two famous examples:

1. **The Courtroom Trial:**

- **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** The defendant is innocent.
- **Alternative Hypothesis ($H_a$):** The defendant is guilty.
- **Type II Error:** Acquitting a **guilty** person. The person actually committed the crime, but the jury failed to find
  enough evidence, letting them walk free.

2. **The Smoke Alarm:**

- **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** There is no fire.
- **Alternative Hypothesis ($H_a$):** There is a fire.
- **Type II Error:** There _is_ a kitchen fire, but your smoke alarm stays completely silent. It failed to alert you to a
  real danger.

---

### The Statistical Tradeoff: Alpha ($\alpha$) vs. Beta ($\beta$)

In statistics, every hypothesis test balances two types of risk, represented by Greek letters:

- **$\alpha$ (Alpha):** The probability of a **Type I error** (false positive).
- **$\beta$ (Beta):** The probability of a **Type II error** (false negative).

There is an inherent tug-of-war between them:

- If you set an extremely strict threshold to avoid false alarms (making $\alpha$ very small, like $0.001$), you make it much
  harder for your test to flag anything. As a result, your risk of a **Type II error ($\beta$) shoots up** because you'll
  start missing real effects.

#### Statistical Power ($1 - \beta$)

The opposite of a Type II error is **statistical power**. Power measures a test's ability to correctly detect a real effect
when it exists. If your test has a power of $0.80$ ($80\%$), it means that if a real effect is out there, your test has an
$80\%$ chance of catching it (and a $20\%$ chance of missing it, resulting in a Type II error). The easiest way to boost
power and lower Type II errors without increasing false positives is to **increase your sample size**.
