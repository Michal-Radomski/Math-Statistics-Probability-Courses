Statistical power and sample size are two fundamental concepts in experimental design. They work together to determine
whether a study has enough data to reliably detect a true effect or relationship without wasting resources.

---

### 1. Statistical Power ($1 - \beta$)

Statistical power is the probability that a study will correctly reject a false null hypothesis—in other words, the chance of
**detecting an effect if it actually exists**.

- **Type II Error ($\beta$):** This is a false negative—failing to find an effect that is really there. Power is calculated
  as $1 - \beta$.
- **Standard Threshold:** Researchers typically aim for a power of **0.80 (or 80%)**, meaning there is an 80% chance of
  detecting a true effect.

### 2. Sample Size ($n$)

Sample size refers to the number of data points, observations, or participants included in a study.

- **Precision:** Larger samples reduce the margin of error and provide a more accurate estimate of the population parameter.
- **Power Relationship:** Holding everything else constant, **increasing the sample size increases statistical power**.

---

### The Four Interlocking Variables

Calculating the required sample size or statistical power involves four interconnected components. If you know any three, you
can solve for the fourth:

1. **Sample Size ($n$):** The number of subjects needed.
2. **Effect Size:** The magnitude of the difference or relationship in the population (e.g., Cohen's $d$). Larger effects are
   easier to spot, requiring smaller samples.
3. **Significance Level ($\alpha$):** The risk of a false positive (Type II error), conventionally set to $0.05$ (5%).
4. **Statistical Power ($1 - \beta$):** The probability of avoiding a false negative, conventionally set to $0.80$ (80%).

---

### Key Trade-offs

- **To decrease sample size** while keeping power constant, you must accept a larger effect size, a higher significance level
  ($\alpha$), or lower power.
- **To detect smaller effect sizes**, you must significantly increase your sample size.
