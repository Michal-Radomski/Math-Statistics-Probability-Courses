In statistics and hypothesis testing, when we analyze data to see if a pattern or effect is real, we always run the risk of
making an incorrect conclusion.

A **Type I error** is a **false positive**. It happens when you incorrectly reject a true null hypothesis ($H_0$), concluding
that a significant effect or relationship exists when, in reality, it happened purely by random chance.

---

### The Classic Analogies

To understand a Type I error, statisticians often use two famous examples:

1. **The Courtroom Trial:**

- **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** The defendant is innocent.
- **Alternative Hypothesis ($H_a$):** The defendant is guilty.
- **Type I Error:** Convicting an **innocent** person. You found a "guilty" signal when no actual crime-committing pattern
  was true for that person.

2. **The Smoke Alarm:**

- **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** There is no fire.
- **Alternative Hypothesis ($H_a$):** There is a fire.
- **Type I Error:** The smoke alarm goes off because you burned toast, even though there is no real fire. It's a false alarm.

---

### Controlling the Risk: The Significance Level ($\alpha$)

We can never completely eliminate the chance of making a Type I error, but we can control its probability using the
**significance level**, represented by the Greek letter **$\alpha$ (alpha)**.

- When a researcher sets $\alpha = 0.05$ (or $5\%$), they are willingly accepting a $5\%$ risk of making a Type I
  error—meaning there is a 1 in 20 chance they will claim to find a significant result when none actually exists.
- To lower the risk of a Type I error, you can make your threshold stricter (e.g., setting $\alpha = 0.01$). However, doing
  this makes it much harder to detect real effects, pushing you toward the opposite mistake: a Type II error.
