**P-hacking** (also known as **data dredging**, **significance chasing**, or **specification searching**) is the misuse of
data analysis to force a study's results to show statistical significance (typically a $p$-value of less than 0.05) where no
real effect actually exists.

In scientific research, a $p$-value helps determine whether a finding is likely a fluke or a real pattern. However, if
researchers test data in multiple ways without a fixed plan, they can easily manufacture a "statistically significant" result
purely by coincidence.

---

### Common Methods of P-Hacking

Researchers might engage in p-hacking—sometimes unintentionally—through several common practices:

- **Optional Stopping:** Collecting data, checking the $p$-value, and deciding to stop collecting more data the moment the
  result dips below 0.05.
- **Torturing the Data:** Testing dozens of different variables, sub-groups, or combinations, but only reporting the one or
  two that yielded a significant correlation.
- **Dropping Outliers:** Removing inconvenient data points or outliers _after_ looking at the results under the justification
  that they "skewed the data," without a pre-established rule for doing so.
- **Controlling for Covariates:** Adding or removing control variables (like age, gender, or income) in a regression model
  until the main variable of interest turns statistically significant.

---

### Why is P-Hacking a Problem?

- **Increases False Positives:** If you run 20 independent statistical tests on completely random data, roughly 1 of them
  will yield a "significant" result ($p < 0.05$) purely by chance. P-hacking dramatically inflates this error rate.
- **Replication Crisis:** Studies driven by p-hacking often cannot be replicated by other scientists, undermining trust in
  scientific literature (particularly in fields like psychology, medicine, and social sciences).
