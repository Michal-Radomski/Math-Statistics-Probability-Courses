A **correlation coefficient** is a statistical measure that calculates both the **strength** and the **direction** of the
relationship between two variables.

While variance, skewness, and kurtosis describe a single variable, correlation looks at how two different things move
together.

---

### The Scale: From -1 to +1

Correlation coefficients are always represented by a number (usually denoted as $r$ for a sample or $\rho$ for a population)
that falls strictly between **-1.0 and +1.0**:

1. **Positive Correlation ($+1.0$):** As one variable goes up, the other variable also goes up. (e.g., Hours spent studying
   and test scores).
2. **Zero Correlation ($0.0$):** There is no linear relationship between the variables at all. Knowing one tells you nothing
   about the other. (e.g., Your shoe size and your grade in history class).
3. **Negative Correlation ($-1.0$):** As one variable goes up, the other variable goes down. (e.g., The number of hours you
   spend playing video games right before an exam and your test score).

---

### Interpreting the Strength of the Coefficient

The further the number is from zero (in either direction), the stronger the relationship.

| Correlation Coefficient ($r$) | Strength & Direction | Example Interpretation                             |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **$+0.7$ to $+1.0$**          | Strong Positive      | Highly predictable upward trend                    |
| **$+0.3$ to $+0.7$**          | Moderate Positive    | A general upward trend, but with plenty of scatter |
| **$0.0$ to $\pm 0.3$**        | Weak or None         | Barely any relationship                            |
| **$-0.3$ to $-0.7$**          | Moderate Negative    | A general downward trend                           |
| **$-0.7$ to $-1.0$**          | Strong Negative      | Highly predictable downward trend                  |

---

### The Golden Rule: Correlation Does Not Equal Causation

Just because two variables have a strong correlation coefficient does not mean that one causes the other.

- _Classic Example:_ Ice cream sales and shark attacks have a strong positive correlation—when ice cream sales go up, so do
  shark attacks.
- _The Reality:_ Ice cream doesn't attract sharks. The hidden third variable (confounding variable) is **summer weather**:
  when it's hot outside, more people eat ice cream, and more people go swimming in the ocean.
