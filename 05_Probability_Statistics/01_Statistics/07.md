**Kurtosis** is a statistical measure that describes the shape of a distribution's tails relative to its peak.

While **variance** measures how wide the spread is and **skewness** measures asymmetry (whether data leans left or right),
**kurtosis measures how likely a dataset is to produce extreme outliers**. It tells you whether the tails of your
distribution are heavy (prone to surprising, extreme values) or thin (data stays close to the center).

---

### The Three Types of Kurtosis

To understand kurtosis, statisticians usually compare a distribution against a standard normal distribution (the classic bell
curve), which has a baseline kurtosis value.

#### 1. Mesokurtic (Normal Tails)

- **What it looks like:** A standard bell curve shape.
- **Characteristics:** It has standard, moderate tails. This serves as the baseline ($0$ excess kurtosis).
- **Example:** Height and weight in large, natural populations.

#### 2. Leptokurtic (Heavy / Fat Tails)

- **What it looks like:** A tall, sharp peak with long, heavy tails stretching out on both sides.
- **Characteristics:** "Lepto" means slender, but don't be fooled—the real story is in the tails. A leptokurtic distribution
  means **extreme, rare events happen much more frequently** than a normal distribution would predict.
- **Example:** Financial market returns. Stocks usually hover near average, but "black swan" market crashes happen more often
  than a normal bell curve suggests.

#### 3. Platykurtic (Thin / Light Tails)

- **What it looks like:** A flat, broad peak with short, thin tails.
- **Characteristics:** "Platy" means flat. This distribution has a lack of extremes—extreme outliers are very rare, and the
  data is tightly bounded or evenly dispersed.
- **Example:** Rolling a six-sided die. Every number has an equal chance of appearing, resulting in a flat distribution with
  no extreme tails.
