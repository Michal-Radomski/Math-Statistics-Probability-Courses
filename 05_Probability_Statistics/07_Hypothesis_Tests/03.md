In hypothesis testing, a **critical region** (also called the **rejection region**) is the set of all possible values of a
test statistic that would lead you to **reject the null hypothesis ($H_0$)**.

Think of it as a statistical "alarm zone." If your sample data produces a test statistic that lands inside this region, the
result is so extreme and unlikely to happen by pure chance alone that you reject the default assumption.

---

### 1. How the Critical Region Works

When you run a hypothesis test, you calculate a test statistic (like a $z$-score or $t$-score) from your sample data. Before
looking at your data, you set up a boundary based on your chosen significance level ($\alpha$):

- **If the test statistic falls inside the critical region:** You reject the null hypothesis.
- **If the test statistic falls outside the critical region (in the "non-rejection region"):** You fail to reject the null
  hypothesis.

---

### 2. What Determines the Critical Region?

The size and location of the critical region depend on two main factors:

1. **The Significance Level ($\alpha$):** This represents your threshold for skepticism—most commonly set at $\mathbf{0.05}$
   (5%). The critical region covers the most extreme $5\%$ of the probability distribution.
2. **The Direction of the Test (One-tailed vs. Two-tailed):**

- **One-Trained Test:** The critical region is entirely in one tail of the distribution (either all on the far right or all
  on the far left), corresponding to a test looking for an increase or decrease only.
- **Two-Trained Test:** The critical region is split evenly between both tails of the distribution (e.g., $2.5\%$ in the left
  tail and $2.5\%$ in the right tail for an $\alpha$ of $0.05$), used when testing for _any_ difference in either direction.

---

### 3. Critical Regions vs. P-Values

Both critical regions and p-values are methods used to make the exact same decision in a hypothesis test, but they look at it
from different angles:

- **Critical Region approach:** You compare your **test statistic** against a **cutoff value** (e.g., _"Is my $z$-score
  greater than $1.96$?"_).
- **P-value approach:** You compare your **probability** against a **significance threshold** (e.g., _"Is my p-value less
