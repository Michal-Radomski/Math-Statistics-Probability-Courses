A **hypothesis test** (or significance test) is a formal statistical method used to test a claim or assumption about a
population based on sample data. It helps researchers decide whether there is enough evidence in the data to support a
specific conclusion, or if the results could have just happened by random chance.

Think of it like a courtroom trial: the status quo is presumed innocent (the default assumption), and the data acts as the
evidence presented to see if we can prove otherwise beyond a reasonable doubt.

---

### 1. The Core Components

Every hypothesis test revolves around two competing statements:

- **The Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** The default assumption that there is **no effect, no difference, or no relationship**
  between variables. (e.g., "This new medicine does not cure acne any better than a placebo.")
- **The Alternative Hypothesis ($H_a$ or $H_1$):** What you are actually trying to prove—that there **is** an effect,
  difference, or relationship. (e.g., "This new medicine _does_ cure acne better than a placebo.")

---

### 2. The Steps of a Hypothesis Test

1. **State the Hypotheses:** Set up your null ($H_0$) and alternative ($H_a$) hypotheses.
2. **Choose a Significance Level ($\alpha$):** Set a threshold for skepticism, commonly denoted as $\alpha$ (alpha). A
   standard choice is **$0.05$ (5%)**, meaning you are willing to accept a 5% chance of falsely claiming an effect exists
   when it doesn't.
3. **Calculate the Test Statistic and P-Value:**

- Run your sample data through a specific formula (like a $z$-test, $t$-test, or chi-square test) to get a test statistic.
- Convert that statistic into a **p-value** (Probability value).

4. **Make a Decision:**

- **If the p-value is less than or equal to $\alpha$ ($p \le 0.05$):** The result is statistically significant. You **reject
  the null hypothesis** in favor of the alternative.
- **If the p-value is greater than $\alpha$ ($p > 0.05$):** There isn't enough evidence. You **fail to reject the null
  hypothesis**.

---

### 3. Understanding the P-Value

The **p-value** is often misunderstood, but it is simply a probability between $0$ and $1$:

- It measures: _"Assuming the null hypothesis is completely true, what is the probability of getting sample data as extreme
  as (or more extreme than) what we just observed?"_
- **Low p-value (e.g., $0.01$):** Your data is extremely unlikely to have happened by pure random chance if the null
  hypothesis were true, so you reject the null.
- **High p-value (e.g., $0.40$):** Your results are very plausible under random chance, so you cannot rule out the null
  hypothesis.

---

### 4. Errors in Hypothesis Testing

Because we are making decisions based on samples rather than the entire population, mistakes can happen:

- **Type I Error (False Positive):** Rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true (e.g., concluding a medicine
  works when it actually does nothing). This is controlled by your $\alpha$ level.
- **Type II Error (False Negative):** Failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is actually false (e.g., concluding a
  medicine doesn't work when it actually does).
