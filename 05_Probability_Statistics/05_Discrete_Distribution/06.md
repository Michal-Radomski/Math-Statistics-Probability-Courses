A **geometric distribution** is a discrete probability distribution that models the **number of trials needed to get the
first success** in a series of independent trials.

While the binomial distribution counts how many successes occur in a _fixed_ number of trials, the geometric distribution
flips the question: the number of trials is _variable_, and we keep going until we hit our first success.

---

### Key Conditions

For a scenario to follow a geometric distribution, it must meet these criteria:

1. **Independent Trials:** Each trial is independent of the others.
2. **Two Possible Outcomes:** Every trial results in either a "success" or a "failure".
3. **Constant Probability ($p$):** The probability of success remains identical for every trial.
4. **Stop at First Success:** The experiment continues until the very first success is achieved, and then it stops.

---

### The Geometric Formula

To find the probability that the **first success happens on the $k$-th trial**, we use the formula:

$$P(X = k) = (1 - p)^{k-1} p$$

Where:

- $k$ = the trial number on which the first success occurs ($1, 2, 3, \dots$)
- $p$ = probability of success on any single trial
- $1 - p$ = probability of failure on any single trial
- $(1 - p)^{k-1}$ = the probability of experiencing $F$ failures in a row before finally getting a success on trial $k$.

---

### Mean and Variance

- **Expected Value (Mean):** $E(X) = \frac{1}{p}$ _(Intuitively, if your chance of success is $1/6$, you expect it to take
  about $6$ trials on average to get your first success.)_
- **Variance:** $\text{Var}(X) = \frac{1-p}{p^2}$
- **Standard Deviation:** $\sigma = \frac{\sqrt{1-p}}{p}$

---

### Step-by-Step Example: Shooting Free Throws

Imagine you are practicing basketball free throws. You are an okay shooter, and your probability of making any single free
throw is **$p = 0.4$** (or $40\%$).

What is the probability that you miss your first two shots and make your **very first basket on your 3rd attempt** ($k = 3$)?

#### 1. Identify the components:

- $p = 0.4$
- $1 - p = 0.6$
- $k = 3$

#### 2. Plug into the formula:

$$P(X = 3) = (0.6)^{3-1} \times 0.4$$

$$P(X = 3) = (0.6)^2 \times 0.4$$

$$P(X = 3) = 0.36 \times 0.4 = 0.144$$

**Interpretation:** There is a **$14.4\%$** chance that your first successful free throw will happen precisely on your third
try.

On average, how many shots should you expect to take to score your first basket?

$$E(X) = \frac{1}{0.4} = 2.5 \text{ attempts}$$
