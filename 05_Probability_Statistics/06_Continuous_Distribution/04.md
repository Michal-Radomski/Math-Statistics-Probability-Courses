A **binomial distribution** is a discrete probability distribution that summarizes the likelihood that a random variable will
take one of two independent values (such as _success/failure_, _yes/no_, or _heads/tails_) across a specific number of
independent trials.

---

### The 4 Conditions of a Binomial Experiment

For a situation to follow a binomial distribution, it must meet four key criteria:

1. **Fixed Number of Trials ($n$):** The experiment is run a predetermined, exact number of times.
2. **Independent Trials:** The outcome of any one trial does not affect or influence the outcome of any other trial.
3. **Two Possible Outcomes:** Every trial results in one of two categories, labeled "success" and "failure".
4. **Constant Probability ($p$):** The probability of success, denoted as $p$, remains exactly the same for every single
   trial. (The probability of failure is $1 - p$).

---

### The Binomial Formula

To find the exact probability of getting $k$ successes out of $n$ trials, we use the binomial probability formula:

$$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$$

Where:

- $n$ = total number of trials
- $k$ = number of successful outcomes we are looking for
- $p$ = probability of success on a single trial
- $(1-p)$ = probability of failure on a single trial
- $\binom{n}{k}$ = "n choose k" (the number of ways to arrange $k$ successes in $n$ trials), calculated as
  $\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$

---

### Mean and Variance of a Binomial Distribution

Because binomial distributions follow strict rules, finding their expected value (mean) and variance is exceptionally
straightforward:

- **Expected Value (Mean):** $\mu = n \cdot p$
- **Variance:** $\sigma^2 = n \cdot p \cdot (1-p)$
- **Standard Deviation:** $\sigma = \sqrt{n \cdot p \cdot (1-p)}$

---

### Example: Taking a Guessing Quiz

Imagine you take a multiple-choice quiz with **5 questions**. Each question has **4 choices**, and you are completely
guessing blindly.

- Total trials ($n$) = $5$
- Probability of guessing any single question right ($p$) = $\frac{1}{4} = 0.25$

If you want to know the **expected number** of questions you will get right just by guessing:

$$E(X) = 5 \times 0.25 = 1.25\text{ questions}$$
