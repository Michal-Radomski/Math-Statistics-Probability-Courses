In probability and statistics, the **Expected Value** (or **expectation**) is the long-term average value you would expect to
get if you repeated a random experiment a massive number of times.

Think of it as the "center of mass" or the weighted average of all possible outcomes, where each outcome is weighted by how
likely it is to happen.

---

### 1. The Core Concept

Even though an expected value often doesn't match any _single_ actual outcome, it tells you what to expect on average over
the long run.

- **Example (Rolling a Die):** If you roll a standard 6-sided die, the possible outcomes are $1, 2, 3, 4, 5,$ and $6$, and
  each has an equal probability of $\frac{1}{6}$.
- To find the expected value, you multiply each outcome by its probability and add them all up:

$$E(X) = (1 \times \frac{1}{6}) + (2 \times \frac{1}{6}) + (3 \times \frac{1}{6}) + (4 \times \frac{1}{6}) + (5 \times \frac{1}{6}) + (6 \times \frac{1}{6}) = 3.5$$

- _Note:_ You can never roll a $3.5$ on a single die toss, but if you roll a die 10,000 times and average the results, the
  average will be extremely close to $3.5$.

---

### 2. Mathematical Formulas

How you calculate the expected value depends on whether your random variable is **discrete** (countable outcomes) or
**continuous** (uncountable range).

#### For Discrete Random Variables:

You sum each possible value ($x$) multiplied by its probability ($P(x)$):

$$E(X) = \sum x \cdot P(x)$$

#### For Continuous Random Variables:

Instead of a sum, you use an integral, multiplying each value ($x$) by the Probability Density Function ($f(x)$):

$$E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \cdot f(x) \, dx$$

---

### 3. Key Properties of Expectation

- **Linearity of Expectation:** One of the most powerful rules in probability is that the expected value of a sum is equal to
  the sum of their individual expected values—**even if the variables are dependent on each other**:

$$E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y)$$

- **Constants:** The expected value of a constant number $c$ is just that constant:

$$E(c) = c$$

- **Scaling:** If you multiply a random variable by a constant, its expected value is multiplied by that same constant:

$$E(cX) = c \cdot E(X)$$

---

### 4. Why Is It Useful?

- **Decision Making & Risk Analysis:** Businesses and insurance companies use expected value to calculate risk. For example,
  if a lottery ticket costs $\$2$ and has a $1$ in $10$ chance of winning a $\$10$ prize, the expected value of the ticket is
  $(10 \times 0.10) = \$1$, meaning on average you lose $\$1$ per ticket.
- **Game Theory:** Helps determine whether a bet, game, or investment is "fair" (where the expected value of net profit is
  zero).
