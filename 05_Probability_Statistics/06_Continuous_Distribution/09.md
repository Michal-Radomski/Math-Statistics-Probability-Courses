A **Probability Density Function (PDF)** is a fundamental concept in statistics and probability used to describe the
likelihood of a _continuous_ random variable taking on a specific value.

While discrete variables (like rolling a die or flipping a coin) have distinct, countable outcomes, continuous variables can
take on an infinite number of values within a given range (such as height, weight, time, or temperature).

---

### 1. The Core Concept: Density vs. Exact Probability

With continuous variables, the probability of the variable landing on an **exact, single point** is technically zero because
there are infinite possibilities (e.g., the exact probability that a person is _precisely_ $1.753829...$ meters tall is $0$).

Instead, a PDF measures **probability density**—how concentrated the probabilities are around a certain region.

- To find the actual probability of an event happening, you look at a **range** rather than a single point (e.g., what is the
  probability that a person's height is between $1.70$ and $1.80$ meters?).
- Graphically, this probability is represented by the **area under the curve** of the PDF within that range.

---

### 2. Key Rules of a PDF

For any function $f(x)$ to be a valid probability density function, it must satisfy two main mathematical rules:

1. **Non-Negativity:** The function can never dip below the x-axis.

$$f(x) \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } x$$

2. **Total Area Equals 1:** The total area under the entire curve of the PDF must equal $1$ (representing 100% total
   probability of all possible outcomes).

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) \, dx = 1$$

---

### 3. How to Calculate Probabilities

To find the probability that a continuous random variable $X$ falls between two values, $a$ and $b$, you calculate the
definite integral of the PDF between those two points:

$$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx$$

- **Higher curve peaks** mean values in that range are more likely to occur.
- **Flatter or lower regions** mean values are less likely to occur.

---

### 4. Common Examples of PDFs

- **The Normal Distribution (Bell Curve):** The most famous PDF, where data clusters symmetrically around a mean, shaped like
  a bell. It is defined by its mean ($\mu$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$).
- **The Uniform Distribution:** A flat, rectangular PDF where every value within a specific range is equally likely to occur
  (like a random number generator picking any decimal between $0$ and $1$).
