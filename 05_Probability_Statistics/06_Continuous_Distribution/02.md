The **normal distribution**—often called the **bell curve** or **Gaussian distribution**—is one of the most important
probability distributions in statistics and mathematics. It describes data that tends to cluster around a central value with
fewer and fewer data points occurring the further you get from the center.

---

## Key Characteristics

1. **Bell-Shaped and Symmetric:** The curve is perfectly symmetrical around its center. If you fold it in half, both sides
   match up identically.
2. **Mean, Median, and Mode are Equal:** In a true normal distribution, the highest point of the curve (the peak) represents
   the **mean** (average), **median** (middle value), and **mode** (most frequent value) all at the exact same point.
3. **Defined by Two Parameters:**

- **Mean ($\mu$):** Determines where the center of the peak is located on the horizontal axis.
- **Standard Deviation ($\sigma$):** Determines how "spread out" or wide the curve is. A small standard deviation creates a
  tall, narrow curve, while a large standard deviation creates a flat, wide curve.

---

## The Empirical Rule (The 68-95-99.7 Rule)

One of the most useful features of a normal distribution is how predictably data spreads out relative to the standard
deviation ($\sigma$) from the mean ($\mu$):

- **68%** of the data falls within **1 standard deviation** of the mean ($\mu \pm 1\sigma$).
- **95%** of the data falls within **2 standard deviations** of the mean ($\mu \pm 2\sigma$).
- **99.7%** of the data falls within **3 standard deviations** of the mean ($\mu \pm 3\sigma$).

This rule makes it easy to spot outliers (data points that lie far outside the norm, typically beyond 2 or 3 standard
deviations).

---

## Real-World Examples

Many natural and social phenomena closely approximate a normal distribution when you collect a large enough sample size, such
as:

- **Human heights and weights** in a given population.
- **Standardized test scores** (like the SAT or IQ tests).
- **Measurement errors** in scientific experiments.
