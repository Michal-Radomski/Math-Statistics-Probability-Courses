A **Z-score** (also known as a **standard score**) tells you exactly how many standard deviations a particular data point is
away from the mean of the distribution.

Instead of looking at raw numbers (like getting 85 on a test or having an IQ of 115), a Z-score translates that data point
into a standardized scale.

---

## The Formula

To calculate the Z-score of any value in a normal distribution, use this formula:

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

- **$x$:** The raw data point or value you are testing.
- **$\mu$ (mu):** The mean of the distribution.
- **$\sigma$ (sigma):** The standard deviation of the distribution.

### What the Result Tells You:

- **$z = 0$:** Your value is exactly at the mean.
- **Positive $z$ (e.g., $+1.5$):** Your value is above the mean (1.5 standard deviations higher).
- **Negative $z$ (e.g., $-2.0$):** Your value is below the mean (2 standard deviations lower).

---

## Why Are Z-Scores Useful?

1. **Apples-to-Apples Comparisons:** Z-scores let you compare completely different datasets. For example, if you scored in
   the 80th percentile on a hard test and someone else scored in the 80th percentile on an easy test, their raw scores might
   look different, but their **Z-scores** let you directly compare how well you both did relative to your respective peers.
2. **Standard Normal Distribution:** When you convert every value in a normal distribution into a Z-score, you transform the
   entire dataset into the **Standard Normal Distribution**, which always has:

- A mean ($\mu$) of **$0$**
- A standard deviation ($\sigma$) of **$1$**

Once data is standardized into Z-scores, statisticians can use a single universal reference table (called a **Z-table**) to
quickly look up probabilities without having to recalculate calculus integrals for every unique mean and standard deviation.
