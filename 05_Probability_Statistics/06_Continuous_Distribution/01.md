A **continuous probability distribution** describes the probabilities of a random variable that can take on _any_ value
within a continuous range or interval (such as real numbers), rather than distinct, isolated steps.

---

### Key Characteristics

- **Infinite Possible Values:** Unlike a discrete distribution where you can count the outcomes (e.g., $1, 2, 3$), a
  continuous variable can take an infinite number of values within any given interval (e.g., any precise height between
  $150.0\text{ cm}$ and $190.0\text{ cm}$).
- **Probability of an Exact Value is Zero:** Because there are infinitely many possible points on a continuous continuum, the
  probability of the random variable taking any _exact_ single value is technically zero ($P(X = x) = 0$). Instead, we
  calculate the probability of the variable falling within a specific _range_.
- **Probability Density Function (PDF):** Continuous distributions use a curve called a PDF. The probability of an event
  occurring within a range is represented by the **area under the curve** for that specific interval.
- **Total Area Equals 1:** Just like discrete distributions, the total area under the entire probability curve must always
  equal $1$ (or $100\%$).

---

### Common Examples

1. **Normal Distribution (The Bell Curve):** The most famous continuous distribution. It models natural phenomena where
   values cluster symmetrically around a central average (mean), such as human heights, weights, or measurement errors.
2. **Uniform Distribution:** A flat distribution where every value within a specific interval has an exact equal chance of
   occurring. For example, picking a random decimal time between $0$ and $60$ minutes.
3. **Exponential Distribution:** Often used to model the amount of time _between_ independent events, such as the time
   waiting for a customer to arrive or the lifespan of an electronic component.
