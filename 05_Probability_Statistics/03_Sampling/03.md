**Random sampling** is a technique used in statistics where every member of a population has an **equal and independent
chance** of being selected for your sample.

Because you can rarely study an entire population directly, random sampling ensures your smaller group is a fair, unbiased
reflection of the whole. If a sample is chosen randomly, you can generalize your findings back to the broader population with
a high degree of confidence.

---

## Key Types of Random Sampling

Depending on the population structure and what you are studying, statisticians use different methods of random selection:

1. **Simple Random Sampling**

- _How it works:_ Every single individual or item has an identical probability of being chosen.
- _Example:_ Putting every student's name in a hat and drawing 50 names blindfolded, or using a computer random number
  generator.

2. **Stratified Random Sampling**

- _How it works:_ The population is divided into subgroups (called "strata") based on shared characteristics (like grade
  level, gender, or income), and a random sample is taken from each subgroup proportionally.
- _Example:_ If a school is 60% freshmen and 40% seniors, a stratified sample of 100 students would randomly pick 60 freshmen
  and 40 seniors.

3. **Systematic Random Sampling**

- _How it works:_ You choose a starting point at random and then pick every $k$-th member (every 10th person, every 50th item
  off an assembly line, etc.) from a sorted list.
- _Example:_ Selecting every 20th customer entering a store for a satisfaction survey.

4. **Cluster Random Sampling**

- _How it works:_ The population is divided into naturally occurring groups or "clusters" (like geographic regions or
  classrooms). You then randomly select entire clusters to study.
- _Example:_ Randomly picking 5 schools out of 50 in a district and surveying every student inside those 5 schools.

---

## Why Random Sampling Matters: Avoiding Bias

The ultimate goal of random sampling is to eliminate **sampling bias**—a systematic error where certain members of a
population are systematically more likely to be chosen than others.

- **Bad Example (Convenience Sampling):** Surveying only your friends about school lunch quality. This introduces heavy bias
  because your friends likely share your opinions and habits.
- **Good Example (Random Sampling):** Using a random number generator to pick 50 students from the entire school roster,
  ensuring athletes, artists, freshmen, and seniors all have a fair shot at being included.
