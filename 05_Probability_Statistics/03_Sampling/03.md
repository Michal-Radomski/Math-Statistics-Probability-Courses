While random sampling gives every member of a population an equal chance of being chosen, **non-random sampling** (also known
as _non-probability sampling_) relies on the researcher's subjective judgment, convenience, or specific criteria rather than
random selection.

Because not everyone has a chance of being selected, non-random samples carry a higher risk of **sampling bias**, meaning the
results might not accurately reflect the entire population. However, they are often faster, cheaper, and more practical for
certain types of research.

---

## Key Types of Non-Random Sampling

Depending on the goals of the study and how accessible the population is, researchers use several common techniques:

1. **Convenience Sampling**

- _How it works:_ Choosing participants who are easiest to reach or readily available.
- _Example:_ Surveying students in your immediate classroom or asking people at a local mall to fill out a questionnaire.
- _The Catch:_ Highly convenient, but heavily biased because it only captures a specific slice of people.

2. **Quota Sampling**

- _How it works:_ The population is divided into subgroups, and the researcher sets a specific "quota" or target number of
  people to collect from each group, without using random selection.
- _Example:_ A market researcher needs to interview 50 men and 50 women, so they just approach the first 50 men and 50 women
  they see.

3. **Purposive (Judgment) Sampling**

- _How it works:_ The researcher deliberately selects participants based on specific traits, expertise, or characteristics
  needed for the study.
- _Example:_ Interviewing expert climate scientists for a documentary about global warming. You aren't trying to represent
  the general public; you want specific, knowledgeable perspectives.

4. **Snowball Sampling**

- _How it works:_ Used when studying hard-to-reach or hidden populations. You find one participant, and that person helps you
  recruit others they know.
- _Example:_ Researching rare medical conditions or underground subcultures where participants are initially difficult to
  find.

---

## When Should You Use Non-Random Sampling?

Even though random sampling is preferred for broad statistical generalizations, non-random sampling is valuable in specific
scenarios:

- **Pilot studies:** Testing a survey or experiment for the first time before doing a large, formal random study.
- **Qualitative research:** When you want deep, detailed insights from specific individuals rather than broad numerical
  averages.
- **Limited resources:** When time, budget, or lack of a complete population list makes true random sampling impossible.
