When data doesn't follow a straight line and isn't purely exponential either, it often bends, curves, rises, and falls in
waves. To model these more complex shapes, we use **polynomial regression**.

---

### What is a Polynomial Model?

While a simple linear regression uses $x$ to the power of 1 ($y = mx + b$), a polynomial model introduces **higher-degree
powers** of $x$ (like $x^2$, $x^3$, or $x^4$) to allow the line to bend.

The general equation looks like this:

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1x + \beta_2x^2 + \beta_3x^3 + \dots + \beta_nx^n$$

- **Quadratic (Degree 2, contains $x^2$):** Creates a single curve (a parabola) that can form a single hill or valley (e.g.,
  shaping like a U or an upside-down U).
- **Cubic (Degree 3, contains $x^3$):** Allows for an "S-shape" curve with both a hill and a valley.
- **Higher Degrees ($x^4$, $x^5$, etc.):** Allow for even more twists and turns to hug the data points tightly.

---

### Real-World Examples

1. **Projectile Motion:** If you throw a ball into the air, its height over time forms an arch. A quadratic model ($x^2$)
   perfectly captures this rise and fall.
2. **Economic and Business Cycles:** Growth that starts slow, accelerates, peaks, and then plateaus or dips often requires a
   polynomial model to capture the shifting phases.

---

### The Danger: Overfitting

While a polynomial model with a high degree (like $x^{10}$) can twist and turn so much that it connects almost every single
dot on your chart, this creates a major statistical trap called **overfitting**.

An overfitted model mistakes random noise for a real pattern. It looks amazing on your past data, but it performs terribly
