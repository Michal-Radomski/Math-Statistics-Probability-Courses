Once you draw a regression line through your data, you can't just assume it's accurate—you have to **evaluate it**.
Evaluating a regression line means checking how well the line fits the data, how reliable its predictions are, and whether
the underlying assumptions of the model hold true.

---

### Key Tools for Evaluating a Regression Line

To determine if your regression model is any good, data scientists and statisticians look at a combination of metrics and
diagnostic plots:

#### 1. The Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$)

- **What it tells you:** The proportion of variance in the dependent variable ($y$) that can be explained by the independent
  variable ($x$).
- **How to read it:** $R^2$ ranges from $0$ to $1$ (or $0\%$ to $100\%$). An $R^2$ of $0.80$ means that $80\%$ of the
  variation in your data is explained by your regression line, leaving $20\%$ as unexplained random noise. Higher is
  generally better, though what counts as a "good" $R^2$ depends entirely on the field (physics models might expect $>0.95$,
  while human behavior studies might consider $0.30$ quite useful).

#### 2. Residual Analysis (Looking at the Errors)

- **What it tells you:** A **residual** is the vertical distance between an actual data point and your predicted regression
  line (the error). By plotting these residuals on a graph, you can see if your model made systematic mistakes.
- **What to look for:** In a good model, residuals should be randomly scattered around zero with no clear pattern. If your
  residual plot shows a curved "U" shape, it means a straight line is the wrong model—you actually need a non-linear curve.

#### 3. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)

- **What it tells you:** The average size of the errors your model makes, measured in the exact same units as your target
  variable.
- **How to read it:** If you are predicting house prices (in thousands of dollars) and your RMSE is $15$, it means your
  model's predictions are typically off by about $\$15,000$.

#### 4. P-Values for the Coefficients

- **What it tells you:** Tests whether the slope of your line is genuinely different from zero, or if it could have happened
  purely by random chance.
- **How to read it:** If the p-value for your slope coefficient is less than $0.05$, you can be confident that your
  independent variable has a statistically significant effect on your dependent variable.
